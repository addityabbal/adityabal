# The Vedas and the Geeta: A Synthesis and Study Guide

*A comprehension guide built from three source books: (1) "Four Vedas – English Translation" (Rig Veda by R.T. Griffith, Yajur Veda by A.B. Keith, Sama Veda by R.T. Griffith, Atharva Veda by M. Bloomfield); (2) "The Vedas – An English-only indexed version of the 4 Veda Samhitas in one document" (Dharmic Scriptures Team, 2002); and (3) Osho's "Geeta Darshan" (the Hindi discourses on the Bhagavad Geeta, 18 chapters, 8 volumes, about 3,360 pages).*

## 0. How this guide was made, and what to trust

Read this section first. It tells you what kind of document this is.

**What the three books are.** Together they are roughly 3,000 pages of English translation and 3,300 pages of Hindi discourse. The two Veda volumes largely carry the same four Samhitas (the collections of hymns and formulas), in two different editions. The Geeta book is not the Geeta text alone: it is Osho's spoken commentary, in Hindi, verse by verse, and it runs far longer than the Geeta itself.

**How I read them.** I could not responsibly claim to have absorbed every page of 6,000+ pages and then compress them into one file. What I did was this:

- I mapped the full structure of each book from its table of contents (how many books, hymns, chapters, and where each begins).
- I read the front matter, prefaces and introductions of the Vedic volumes.
- I read in full the best-known and most thematically central hymns (plus several ordinary ones, in Section 8A): the Hymn of the Primeval Man (Purusha Sukta, 10.90), the Hymn of Creation (Nasadiya, 10.129), the hymn to Ka / Hiranyagarbha (10.121), Savitar hymns including the Gayatri verse, the riddle hymn 1.164, the Tryambaka verse, the opening of the Yajur Veda, the Rudra litany of the Yajur Veda, the opening decades of the Sama Veda, the Atharva Veda's Hymn to Earth (12.1) and its two Hymns to Time (19.53, 19.54), and the grouped contents of the Atharva Veda's ten categories.
- For Osho's Geeta Darshan I read the opening of each of the 18 chapter-discourses, and I read longer passages from several of them (the senses and suppression, the not-doing story, firmness of mind, the Gunas at the end).
- Everything in this guide that comes from a source passage I actually read is flagged as such where it matters. Where I am drawing on general knowledge of the Geeta's own text (the verses of the Geeta are short, well known, and not Osho's), I say so.

**Known limits.**

1. The Hindi PDF's text layer is imperfect. Some conjunct characters drop out, so some sentences come through broken. I could follow the sense of what I read, but I may have missed fine points, and I do not quote Osho's exact Hindi wording. When I report what Osho says, it is my paraphrase in English.
2. For a few of Osho's chapters (notably Chapter 8) I read the verse text and the chapter opening but not enough of the discourse to say much about Osho's own argument. I mark those clearly.
3. I did not read every hymn of the Rig Veda's 1,000-plus hymns, every verse of the Yajur Veda's ritual formulas, or every Atharva Veda charm. The summaries of those parts are built from the structure, the headings, the introductions, and the sampled hymns. They are accurate as maps. They are not claims of line-by-line knowledge.
4. The two Veda books are early-20th-century and late-19th-century translations (Griffith 1889-1893, Keith 1914, Bloomfield 1897), plus a 2002 compiled edition that mixes those with newer re-translations. Their wording is dated and in places scholars now translate differently. The 2002 compilation has an openly devotional and programmatic introduction; I describe it neutrally below.

**How to use this guide.** Part A is the Vedas. Part B is the Geeta through Osho's eyes. Part C is where the two meet. Part D is reference: glossary, comparisons, study questions, and a reading plan. # PART A: THE VEDAS

## 1. What the Vedas are

"Veda" comes from the root *vid*, to know. The Vedas are the oldest layer of Sanskrit literature and the root texts of the Indian tradition. The word covers more than the four collections, but the "Veda" in the narrow sense is the four **Samhitas**, the compiled collections of mantras:

| Veda | Character | Main function | Edition used here |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Rig Veda** | Hymns of praise (*rik*, verse) | Recited praise to the gods; the oldest, most poetic layer | Griffith (10 "Books" or Mandalas) |
| **Yajur Veda** | Sacrificial formulas (*yajus*) | The prose-and-verse formulas spoken by the officiating priest during the ritual | Keith's translation of the Black (Taittiriya) school |
| **Sama Veda** | Melodies (*saman*) | Verses set to chant, mostly drawn from the Rig Veda | Griffith |
| **Atharva Veda** | Spells, charms, healing, statecraft, philosophy | Domestic and practical life; also some of the most speculative hymns | Bloomfield's selection, grouped by theme |

A useful way to remember the division of labour in a Vedic sacrifice: the *hotar* priest recited Rig verses, the *adhvaryu* priest performed the actions while muttering Yajus formulas, the *udgatar* priest sang Sama chants, and the *brahman* priest supervised the whole thing, traditionally associated with the Atharva Veda. The Rig is the words, the Yajur is the act, the Sama is the song, and the Atharva is the safeguard and the everyday.

**Layers of Vedic literature.** The Samhitas are only the first layer. After them come the *Brahmanas* (ritual explanation), the *Aranyakas* (forest texts, more interior), and the *Upanishads* (philosophical culmination, called *Vedanta*, "the end of the Veda"). Neither Veda volume here includes those later layers; the Samhitas are the poetry and the ritual formulas. This matters for the Geeta, because the Geeta is a text in the Vedanta stream, built partly on Upanishadic ideas, and it comments critically on the Vedic ritual religion that the Samhitas record. That relationship is the spine of Part C.

**Tradition about authorship.** The tradition calls the Vedas *shruti*, "that which was heard," meaning revealed to seers (*rishis*) rather than composed in the ordinary sense. The 2002 compilation lists ten priestly families who "wrote" the Rig Veda's ten books: the Kanvas, Angirases, Agastyas, Gritsamadas, Vishvamitras, Atris, Vasishthas, Kashyapas, Bharatas, and Bhrigus. (Scholars usually describe the family books as books 2 through 7 belonging to Gritsamada, Vishvamitra, Vamadeva, Atri, Bharadvaja and Vasishtha, with books 1, 8, 9 and 10 compiled from various sources. The compilation's list is its own scheme.)

## 2. The two editions in your files, and how they differ

**Book 1, "Four Vedas – English Translation".** About 1,446 pages in one PDF. It is a straight bundle of four older scholarly translations:

- The **Rig Veda** (Griffith), ten books, around 1,028 hymns in all. Hymns are titled by deity: "Hymn I. Agni," "Hymn II. Vayu," and so on.
- The **Yajur Veda** in the Black Yajus school (Taittiriya Samhita), translated by A.B. Keith, organized in seven *kandas* with *prapathakas* (sections), and numbered by verse in the style "i. 1. 1." Pages roughly 616 to 1175 of the file. It carries sub-headings for the rite being described: "The New and Full Moon Sacrifices," "The Soma Sacrifice," "The Victim for Agni and Soma," "The Soma Cups," "The Offerings to Rudra," "The Horse Sacrifice," "The Gavam Ayana," and so on.
- The **Sama Veda** (Griffith, 1893), in two parts: a First Part of six books (each in two chapters), made of "decades" of ten verses, and a Second Part of nine books (each in three chapters). It has a preface by Griffith that I summarize in Section 9.
- The **Atharva Veda** (Bloomfield), not a full translation but a topical selection of hymns, grouped into ten categories (see Section 10), with an excerpt from Bloomfield's introduction.

**Book 2, "The Vedas, English-only, indexed" (Dharmic Scriptures Team, 2002).** About 1,109 pages, labeled "Issue 1, Draft 2." It reorganizes the same four Vedas into a single numbering scheme (for example, "1.10.129" means Rig Veda, Mandala 10, Sukta 129) and adds an index. Its introduction states three goals: to gather all Dharmic scriptures online, to eventually distil a unified book for practitioners, and to support what it calls a global Dharmic renaissance. For the Rig Veda it keeps Griffith's 1895 text for most verses but replaces specific verses (it lists them, for instance 1.1.1-3, 1.1.7, 1.1.9, 10.121.1-3, 10.129.1-3 and 6-7, 10.190, and many others) with newer re-translations, which in its words are "more meaningful" where Griffith was "more literal." It also contains a short overview section for each Veda.

Three differences to keep in mind when you cross-reference the two:

1. **Numbering.** Book 1 uses book-and-hymn headings; Book 2 uses Mandala.Sukta.Mantra. A reference like "1.10.90" in Book 2 corresponds to "Book the Tenth, Hymn XC" in Book 1.
2. **Translation voice.** Where Book 2 has swapped in new wording, it reads more devotional and interpretive. Compare 1.1.1: the older Griffith version calls Agni "the chosen Priest, God, minister of sacrifice, the hotar, lavishest of wealth." Book 2 renders it "I glorify Agni, the divine Priest and the messenger of my oblations to God who is the bestower of prosperity." The second reading folds the poem toward monotheistic devotion, which is a choice of the compilers, not a neutral fact about the Sanskrit.
3. **Counts.** Book 2's introduction gives per-Mandala hymn counts (191, 48, 69, 58, 87, 84, 101, 103, 114, 191 in its list, with a note that the English-translated counts differ in some Mandalas) and a total of 1,046 hymns in one place and 1,028 "Mantras" in another. The standard count for the Rig Veda is around 1,028 hymns and about 10,500 verses. For the Yajur Veda Book 2 states six kandhas, while the Taittiriya Samhita as translated by Keith in Book 1 is organized in seven kandas. For the Sama Veda it gives 1,875 mantras (with a bracketed question mark at 1,800) in two parts: a Purvarchika of about 650 and an Uttararchika of about 1,225. For the Atharva Veda it speculates that the whole tradition may total as many as 12,300 hymns when all recensions and supplements are added, while allotting only 1,320 to its own draft. These are the compilers' working figures; treat the counts as approximate.

## 3. The Vedic world: what is going on in these hymns

If you open the Rig Veda expecting abstract philosophy, you will be surprised. The bulk of it is a conversation between a ritual community and powerful natural and cosmic forces they treat as persons. A rough sketch of that world:

- **A pastoral, warrior, ritual society.** The priests ask for cattle, horses, sons, long life, victory over enemies, and rain. The gods are asked for *rayi* (wealth), *vira* (heroic offspring), and *svasti* (well-being).
- **The sacrifice (*yajna*) as the engine of the cosmos.** Humans feed the gods with offerings placed in fire; the gods in return sustain the world. The exchange is not bribery. It is participation in cosmic order.
- **The cosmic order (*rita*).** This is the single most important concept. Rita is the lawful regularity of the universe: sunrise, the seasons, the flow of rivers, the moral truthfulness that keeps society and nature in alignment. It is the ancestor of the later word *dharma*. The Varuna hymns are especially about rita and its moral breach.
- **Soma.** A ritual plant-drink pressed, filtered and offered, celebrated especially in the whole ninth book of the Rig Veda ("Soma Pavamana," Soma being purified). It is a god, a plant, a drink, and a symbol of inspiration all at once.
- **A fluid, shifting set of gods.** The same hymn can address Agni as the supreme god, then another hymn does the same for Indra or Varuna. Max Müller named this feature "kathenotheism" or "henotheism": each deity is treated as supreme in the moment of address. Other hymns, as we will see, go further and say the many are one.

### The principal deities

- **Agni** (fire): the priest, the messenger, the mouth of the gods, the one who carries offerings to heaven. The Rig Veda opens with him (1.1), and the Sama Veda's first decades begin with him too. He is also the fire in the home, the fire in the body, and the fire of insight.
- **Indra**: the warrior-king of the gods, wielder of the thunderbolt, slayer of the dragon Vritra who held back the waters. About a quarter of the Rig Veda is addressed to him. He is the god of the rainstorm, of battle, and of the exhilaration of Soma.
- **Soma**: the plant, the drink, the god of the whole ninth Mandala.
- **Varuna and Mitra**: guardians of rita and of oaths. Varuna sees everything, and the hymns to him include some of the most moral and penitential verses in the collection.
- **Surya and Savitar**: the sun as visible god, and the sun as impeller, the "stimulator" who drives the world into motion each morning. The famous Gayatri verse is addressed to Savitar.
- **Ushas** (Dawn): one of the most beautiful figures in the Rig Veda, a young woman of light who wakes the world.
- **The Ashvins**: twin divine physicians and horsemen who rescue the distressed.
- **The Maruts**: storm-gods, Indra's troop. **Rudra**: the fierce, howling storm-god of arrows and healing herbs, who will become Shiva. **Vayu**: wind. **Vishnu**: the one who strides across the world in three steps; a minor figure in the Rig Veda who will become one of the great gods of later Hinduism.
- **Prithivi and Dyaus**: Earth and Heaven, the parents of the world.
- **Vishvedevas**: "All the gods," a category for hymns addressed to the gods collectively.
- **Abstract powers:** Faith (Shraddha), Desire (Kama), Time (Kala), Breath (Prana), Speech (Vac), the Primeval Man (Purusha), the Golden Germ (Hiranyagarbha), the Support (Skambha). These abstract and cosmogonic figures are where the Vedas begin to turn toward philosophy.

### Words you will meet constantly

*Rita*, the cosmic order; *yajna*, sacrifice; *mantra*, a sacred verse or formula; *sukta*, a hymn ("well-said"); *rishi*, a seer; *soma*, the sacred drink; *tapas*, heat, creative fervour, austerity; *brahman* (neuter), the sacred power inherent in the sacred utterance, later the Absolute; *atman*, the self; *asat* and *sat*, non-being and being; *asura* and *deva*, the two classes of powerful beings (in the early hymns *asura* can be honourable, as with Varuna; later it means demon).

## 4. How the Vedas move from ritual to philosophy

The most important single thing to understand about the Vedas, if you are reading them with the Geeta in mind, is that they are not one voice. The collection runs from ritual petition to cosmic speculation, and one can watch the shift inside the Rig Veda's own pages. In the earlier family books (2 through 7), the hymns are mostly about praising gods and requesting goods. In the tenth and latest Mandala, new questions appear: where did everything come from? what is the one behind the many? what is death? what is the nature of sacrifice itself? The Atharva Veda continues this with its hymns to Time, Breath, Desire and the Support.

So "the Vedic religion" is not a single teaching. You could describe it as three overlapping currents:

1. **Petition and praise** (most of the Rig Veda): honour the powers and ask for life's goods.
2. **Ritual technology** (most of the Yajur Veda and the Sama Veda): get the sacrifice exactly right, because the right action and the right sound maintain the cosmos.
3. **Speculation and mystery** (parts of Rig Mandala 1 and 10, parts of the Atharva Veda): there is one reality behind the gods; the cosmos is a single body, a single sacrifice, or a single breath.

The Geeta takes a position inside this inheritance. It affirms the third current, reinterprets the second, and criticizes the first when taken alone.

## 5. The Rig Veda: structure and character

The Rig Veda is the heart of the Vedic corpus and the oldest of the four. It has **ten Mandalas** (circles, or "Books"). In Griffith's translation (Book 1 in your files) each Mandala is a "Book" and each hymn is titled by its deity.

**Shape of the ten books, as the files present them.**

- **Book 1** is the longest, 191 hymns, and the most varied. It opens with Agni (1.1), contains the great Savitar hymn, and includes the famous riddle-hymns of the seer Dirghatamas (1.164, "the hymn of the year and the riddles") that drift into philosophy.
- **Books 2 to 7** are the "family books," each ascribed to one priestly clan: about 43 to 62 hymns in books 2 and 3, and the larger books 4, 5, 6, 7. Their poems are mostly addressed to Agni, Indra and the other great gods and contain the oldest, most ritual-centred material. (Book 2's hymn count is 43 in Griffith; the 2002 compilation lists 48 "Sanskrit hymns" with 43 translated, a difference it flags.)
- **Book 8** (about 103 hymns) is the book of the Kanvas, with many shorter hymns to Indra and the Ashvins.
- **Book 9** (114 hymns) is entirely to **Soma Pavamana**, "Soma being purified," and describes the pressing and filtering of the sacred juice through the woollen sieve into the vat.
- **Book 10** (191 hymns) is the latest and the most speculative. It contains the Purusha hymn, the Creation hymn, the hymn to the Golden Germ, hymns on Faith, on Dream-charms, on the Dice Gambler, on the funeral and the dead (Yama), on marriage (the Suryā hymn, 10.85), and the dialogue of Yama and Yami. (The playful Hymn of the Frogs is in Book 7.)

**Two ways to read it.** One is historical: the hymns reflect the life of Vedic-speaking communities of the northwest of the subcontinent in the second millennium BCE. The other is as a living scripture: the tradition has read the same hymns as symbolic and spiritual. Both readings have been taken seriously by scholars in India and the West, and the translations in your two files lean toward the first (Griffith) and toward a devotional version of the second (2002 compilation).

**What the hymns sound like.** The poems are compact, intense and often riddling. A typical hymn invokes a deity, celebrates a deed, makes a request, and closes with a refrain asking for wealth and heroes. But the quality of the poetry is high, and the best of them anticipate everything the Upanishads and the Geeta will develop later.

## 6. Seven landmark hymns, read closely

I read these in full in the Griffith translation (Book 1 of your files). The line quotations below are from that translation.

### 6.1 Rig Veda 1.1: Agni, the first hymn

> "I laud Agni, the chosen Priest, God, minister of sacrifice, the hotar, lavishest of wealth."

The collection opens with fire. Agni is simultaneously a god, the ritual fire on the altar, and a priest: he is the *messenger* who carries human offerings to the gods and brings the gods to the sacrifice. The hymn's logic is the logic of the entire Vedic world: "Through Agni man obtaineth wealth, yea, plenty waxing day by day, most rich in heroes, glorious." The offering that goes into the fire goes to the gods, and the gods return benefits. The hymn ends on a hope for closeness: "Be to us easy of approach, as is a father to his son." The Sama Veda, as we will see, also begins with Agni, and the Gita (as discussed in Part C) reinterprets this entire fire-and-offering relationship.

### 6.2 Rig Veda 10.129: The Hymn of Creation (*Nasadiya Sukta*)

This is the most celebrated philosophical poem in the Vedas. It is only seven verses long. The tone is not that of a revealed doctrine but of a questioning poet.

> "Then was not non-existent nor existent: there was no realm of air, no sky beyond it. What covered in, and where? and what gave shelter? Was water there, unfathomed depth of water?
> Death was not then, nor was there aught immortal: no sign was there, the day's and night's divider. That One Thing, breathless, breathed by its own nature: apart from it was nothing whatsoever."

The sequence of ideas, verse by verse:

1. **Before the beginning.** Neither being nor non-being. No air, no sky, only an undefined "water" of potential.
2. **The One.** "That One Thing, breathless, breathed by its own nature." The only thing in the poem's pre-cosmos is a single self-sustaining something, breathing without breath. It is not described as a person or a god.
3. **Darkness and heat.** "Darkness there was: at first concealed in darkness this All was indiscriminated chaos. All that existed then was void and formless: by the great power of Warmth was born that Unit." *Tapas*, heat or fervour, is the generative energy.
4. **Desire.** "Thereafter rose Desire in the beginning, Desire, the primal seed and germ of Spirit. Sages who searched with their heart's thought discovered the existent's kinship in the non-existent." *Kama* is the first movement of mind within the One, and it is the seed of mind.
5. **The tiers.** "Transversely was their severing line extended: what was above it then, and what below it?" The cosmos separates into high and low.
6. **The gods are late.** "The Gods are later than this world's production. Who knows then whence it first came into being?" This is a remarkable line: the gods of the sacrifice are themselves products, not origins.
7. **The honest ending.** "He, the first origin of this creation, whether he formed it all or did not form it, whose eye controls this world in highest heaven, he verily knows it, or perhaps he knows not."

The poem ends in open uncertainty, even about whether the overseer himself knows. For readers of the Geeta, this is the Vedic ancestor of the Geeta's insistence that the ultimate is beyond ordinary categories of being and non-being. When the Geeta says (13.12) that the beginningless Supreme Brahman "is said to be neither being nor non-being," it is echoing this very hymn.

### 6.3 Rig Veda 10.90: The Hymn of the Primeval Man (*Purusha Sukta*)

This is the Vedic cosmogony by sacrifice. It is also the most-quoted and most-disputed hymn in the Rig Veda, because verse 12 is the earliest textual mention of the four social classes.

> "A thousand heads hath Purusa, a thousand eyes, a thousand feet. On every side pervading earth he fills a space ten fingers wide.
> This Purusa is all that yet hath been and all that is to be; the Lord of Immortality which waxes greater still by food.
> So mighty is his greatness; yea, greater than this is Purusa. All creatures are one-fourth of him, three-fourths eternal life in heaven."

The structure is as follows.

- **Verses 1 to 5:** Purusha is a cosmic being who is the whole universe and also more than it. One quarter of him is the visible world; three quarters are "eternal life in heaven." This 1:3 relation between the manifest and the transcendent will resonate with the Geeta's 10.42, where Krishna says he pervades the whole universe "with a single fragment of himself."
- **Verses 6 to 9:** The gods perform a sacrifice in which Purusha himself is the victim. From that offering come the seasons, the animals, and the sacred texts themselves: "From that great general sacrifice Rcas and Sama-hymns were born: therefrom were spells and charms produced; the Yajus had its birth from it." In other words, the Vedas themselves are produced by the primal sacrifice.
- **Verses 11 to 12:** "When they divided Purusa how many portions did they make? What do they call his mouth, his arms? What do they call his thighs and feet? The Brahman was his mouth, of both his arms was the Rajanya made. His thighs became the Vaisya, from his feet the Sudra was produced." This verse gives a mythic image of society as a body of four orders. Hindu tradition has read it in two quite different ways: as a charter for a hierarchy fixed by birth, and as an organic metaphor for interdependent functions. Modern scholars note that this verse is often thought to be a late addition to the hymn, and that the term *varna* in the earlier hymns of the Rig Veda is not a clear hereditary caste system. The reader should note that the Geeta also refers to the four orders (4.13, 18.41-44) and that Osho, who is hostile to birth-based hierarchy elsewhere in his work, has his own view. I did not read Osho's discourse on those particular verses closely enough to report what he says here.
- **Verses 13 to 14:** "The Moon was gendered from his mind, and from his eye the Sun had birth; Indra and Agni from his mouth were born, and Vayu from his breath. Forth from his navel came mid-air; the sky was fashioned from his head, earth from his feet, and from his ear the regions. Thus they formed the worlds."
- **Verse 16:** "Gods, sacrificing, sacrificed the victim: these were the earliest holy ordinances. The Mighty Ones attained the height of heaven, there where the Sadhyas, Gods of old, are dwelling."

**Why this hymn matters for the Geeta.** It gives three ideas that the Geeta takes up and transforms. First, the cosmos *is* a person, not a heap: the Geeta's eleventh chapter, the Vision of the Universal Form, is a personal, dramatic version of the same idea. Second, creation is a *sacrifice*: the Geeta (3.14-16) repeats that the cosmos is sustained by a wheel of sacrifice. Third, the word "Purusha" becomes, in Geeta 15.16-19, the "Purushottama," the Supreme Person, who exceeds both the perishable and the imperishable.

### 6.4 Rig Veda 10.121: To Ka, the Unknown God (*Hiranyagarbha*)

This hymn has a refrain in every verse, "What God shall we adore with our oblation?" (the Sanskrit "kasmai devaya havisha vidhema," where *ka* means both "who?" and "Ka" as a name). Its opening:

> "In the beginning rose Hiranyagarbha, born only Lord of all created beings. He fixed and holdeth up this earth and heaven. What God shall we adore with our oblation?"

Here the Golden Germ (or Golden Egg) is the first-born, who becomes the upholder of heaven and earth. The poem describes him as the giver of breath and power whose commands all the gods acknowledge, and "the Lord of death, whose shade is life immortal." Its last verse names him: "Prajapati! Thou only comprehendest all these created things, and none beside thee." The hymn dramatizes the move from many gods toward a single lord, and the question in its refrain, asked of every verse, is both devotional and sceptical. The 2002 compilation chose to retranslate verses 1, 2, 3, 5 and 10.

### 6.5 Rig Veda 1.164: The riddle hymn of the seer Dirghatamas

This is the most puzzling long hymn in the first book, 52 verses of riddles, and it contains three of the most-quoted lines in the Vedas.

- Verse 20, **the Two Birds:** "Two Birds with fair wings, knit with bonds of friendship, in the same sheltering tree have found a refuge. One of the twain eats the sweet fig-tree's fruitage; the other eating not regardeth only." This image became, in the later Mundaka and Shvetashvatara Upanishads, the classic picture of the individual self who tastes the fruit of action and the Witness who merely watches. It is the seed of the Geeta's distinction between the one who acts (the *kshetra*, field) and the Knower of the field (the *kshetrajna*, chapter 13), and of the Witness (*sakshi*) that Osho presses on constantly.
- Verse 45, **the four parts of speech:** "Speech hath been measured out in four divisions, the Brahmans who have understanding know them. Three kept in close concealment cause no motion; of speech, men speak only the fourth division." Three quarters of language is hidden; ordinary speech is the fourth. Compare the Purusha Sukta's three quarters hidden and one quarter manifest.
- Verse 46, **the One and its many names:** "They call him Indra, Mitra, Varuna, Agni, and he is heavenly nobly-winged Garutman. To what is One, sages give many a title: they call it Agni, Yama, Matarisvan." This is the clearest early statement that the many gods are many names of one reality, *ekam sad viprā bahudhā vadanti*. Whatever else one says about "Vedic polytheism," the Vedas themselves contain this sentence.

The hymn also has the verse of honest confusion: "What thing I truly am I know not clearly: mysterious, fettered in my mind I wander" (verse 37). The speaker admits he does not know his own nature. That, again, is the existential posture of Arjuna at the start of the Geeta.

### 6.6 Savitar, the Gayatri verse, and the theme of illumination

I read the Savitar hymns (1.35 and others). Savitar is the sun as *impeller*, the one who sets the world in motion each dawn. In 1.35: "Borne in his golden chariot he cometh, Savitar, God who looks on every creature." He is "the golden-handed" who "drives away sickness."

The most famous verse in the Vedas is a Savitar verse, the Gayatri mantra (Rig Veda 3.62.10). Griffith renders 3.62.10 as "May we attain that excellent glory of Savitar the God: so may he stimulate our prayers." Its significance is that the prayer is not for cattle or sons but for *dhi*, insight or understanding: "may he impel our thoughts." The Geeta explicitly honours it: "Among metres I am the Gayatri" (10.35). In Osho's fourth chapter, the same imagery returns: Krishna says he first taught the eternal yoga to the sun, and Osho reads the Sun as a symbol of light, the first and last event of spiritual experience (see Part B).

### 6.7 Rig Veda 7.59.12: the Tryambaka verse

> "Tryambaka we worship, sweet augmenter of prosperity. As from its stem the cucumber, so may I be released from death, not reft of immortality."

Addressed to Rudra in the three-eyed or three-mothered form. The image of the ripe cucumber detaching naturally from its vine, painlessly, is one of the most graceful images of death in all Indian literature. This verse (the *Mahamrityunjaya* mantra) is chanted to this day. It shows that the Vedic poets already wanted not merely long life but release from the fear of death. The Geeta's treatment of death and the deathless self (2.13, 2.20, 2.27) belongs to this same line of thought.

## 7. The Rig Veda by theme (how to find your way around)

If you want to search the Rig Veda, here are the themes to look for.

- **Cosmology and creation:** 10.129 (Nasadiya), 10.90 (Purusha), 10.121 (Hiranyagarbha), 10.72 (the birth of the gods), 10.81 and 10.82 (Vishvakarman, the All-Maker).
- **Moral order:** the Varuna hymns, especially 7.86-89, and 1.24-25, in which the poet pleads for pardon for sin and begs release from "the bonds."
- **Death and the afterlife:** 10.14-18 (Yama, the fathers, the funeral hymn).
- **Language, knowledge, and speech:** 10.71 (the hymn to Brihaspati as lord of speech and the story of the origin of speech), 10.125 (the Vak hymn, where Speech herself speaks as the cosmic power).
- **Social and human life:** 10.34 (the gambler's lament), 10.85 (the marriage hymn), 10.117 (hymn on giving: "the man who eats alone eats sin").
- **Dialogues and stories:** Yama and Yami (10.10), Pururavas and Urvashi (10.95), Sarama and the Panis (10.108).
- **Everyday and nature:** the Ushas hymns, Parjanya (rain), the Frogs (7.103), the Forest (10.146), the Rivers (the Nadistuti, 10.75).

## 8. The Rig Veda in one page

If someone asks you what the Rig Veda *is*, you could say:

> A collection of about a thousand hymns in ten books, composed over several centuries, praising and petitioning gods who are personified powers of nature and ritual (Agni, Indra, Soma, Varuna, the Sun, the Dawn). It treats a fire sacrifice as the way humans and gods sustain one another and the cosmic order (*rita*). Its latest hymns turn to philosophical questions: what was before creation (10.129), what is the One behind the many gods (1.164.46), how does the universe come from a primal sacrifice (10.90), and how can a mortal find release from death (7.59.12). Many of the hymns are also genuinely beautiful nature poetry.

## 8A. More hymns, read in Griffith's translation

The landmark hymns above are the philosophical peaks. To get the *texture* of the Rig Veda you also need the ordinary hymns, which are the bulk of the collection. I read these in full or in large part. Together they show the range from sin and repentance to storm-battle, from dawn-poetry to the gambler's lament.

### 8A.1 Indra slays the Dragon (1.32): the founding myth

> "I will declare the manly deeds of Indra, the first that he achieved, the Thunder-wielder. He slew the Dragon, then disclosed the waters, and cleft the channels of the mountain torrents… Impetuous as a bull, he chose the Soma and in three sacred beakers drank the juices. Maghavan grasped the thunder for his weapon, and smote to death this firstborn of the dragons… Then, giving life to Sun and Dawn and Heaven, thou foundest not one foe to stand against thee."

This is the myth at the centre of Vedic religion. The dragon (Vritra, "the Encloser") has held back the waters in the mountain; Indra, strengthened by Soma and armed with the thunderbolt fashioned by Tvashtar, splits him open and the rivers run to the sea, and sun and dawn can come to life. It is a nature myth (the monsoon or the spring melt), a cosmic myth (order defeating chaos), and a *model for ritual*: each sacrifice re-enacts Indra's act, releasing waters and light by drinking Soma and singing his praises. The Geeta's Krishna, who is both warrior's charioteer and the Lord of all, stands in the line of this combat tradition: "Of the gods I am Indra" (10.22), and the Geeta's war is introduced as a *dharma-yuddha*. But notice what Arjuna is asked to do differently from Indra: he is not asked to enjoy the victory.

### 8A.2 Varuna and the sense of sin (7.86): a hymn of repentance

Not all of the Rig Veda is about winning. Varuna's hymns are among the most inward. The poet in 7.86 is troubled:

> "With mine own heart I commune on the question how Varuna and I may be united. What gift of mine will he accept unangered? When may I calmly look and find him gracious? Fain to know this sin I question others: I seek the wise, O Varuna, and ask them. This one same answer even the sages gave me, 'Surely this Varuna is angry with thee.'
> What, Varuna, hath been my chief transgression, that thou wouldst slay the friend who sings thy praises? Tell me, Unconquerable Lord, and quickly sinless will I approach thee with mine homage. Free us from sins committed by our fathers, from those wherein we have ourselves offended…
> Not our own will betrayed us, but seduction, thoughtlessness, Varuna, wine, dice, or anger. The old is near to lead astray the younger: even sleep removeth not all evil-doing."

Three things stand out. First, the poet *asks the wise* and gets the same answer from everyone, so there is already a community of moral advice. Second, he assigns his wrongdoing to *seduction, thoughtlessness, wine, dice and anger*, not to a free decision, which anticipates the Geeta's chapter 3 diagnosis of desire and anger as the enemies that drive people "as if by force" (3.36-37). Third, he ends with a plan: "Slavelike may I do service to the Bounteous… This gentle Lord gives wisdom to the simple." Here is a seed of *bhakti*, the kind of surrender that 18.66 will name.

### 8A.3 Ushas, the Dawn (1.113)

> "This light is come, amid all lights the fairest; born is the brilliant, far-extending brightness. Night, sent away for Savitar's uprising, hath yielded up a birth-place for the Morning… Common, unending is the Sisters' pathway; taught by the Gods, alternately they travel. Fair-formed, of different hues and yet one-minded, Night and Dawn clash not, neither do they travel… She, stirring up the world, hath shown us riches: Dawn hath awakened every living creature."

The Dawn hymns are among the oldest nature poetry in the world. Here the idea of *rita* appears in the beautiful form of two sisters, Night and Dawn, who take turns on one road without ever clashing. The poem sees the day-night rhythm as the sign of an order that holds the whole cosmos. It also shows the Vedic temperament at its most positive: the world as a gift, waking as a blessing.

### 8A.4 Soma and the longing for immortality (9.113)

The ninth Mandala consists of hymns to the pressing and purifying of Soma. One of its most remarkable hymns (9.113) is a *journey of the soul*, with a refrain "Flow, Indu, flow for Indra's sake," and a series of prayers, each opening "Make me immortal in that realm…":

> "Make me immortal in that realm where dwells the King, Vivasvan's Son… Make me immortal in that realm where they move even as they list, in the third sphere of inmost heaven where lucid worlds are full of light… Make me immortal in that realm of eager wish and strong desire, the region of the radiant Moon, where food and full delight are found."

This is the Vedic idea of the highest heaven: a realm of light, freedom of movement, and fulfilled desire. Here is exactly the "heaven of Indra" that Krishna describes in the Geeta (9.20-21) as the reward of the Soma-drinking Vedic sacrificer, and exactly the thing the Geeta says is not final: a place one *returns* from when merit runs out. When the Geeta says "worlds up to the realm of Brahma are subject to return, but whoever reaches me does not return" (8.16), it is saying that even this lovely heaven is a stage.

### 8A.5 Vak, the Goddess of Speech speaks (10.125)

> "I travel with the Rudras and the Vasus, with the Adityas and All-Gods I wander. I hold aloft both Varuna and Mitra, Indra and Agni, and the Pair of Asvins… I am the Queen, the gatherer-up of treasures, most thoughtful, first of those who merit worship… Through me alone all eat the food that feeds them, each man who sees, breathes, hears the word outspoken. They know it not, but yet they dwell beside me. Hear, one and all, the truth as I declare it… I make the man I love exceeding mighty, make him a sage, a Rishi, and a Brahman… I breathe a strong breath like the wind and tempest, the while I hold together all existence."

This is a rare hymn in which the divine *speaks in the first person*, as the Geeta's Krishna will. Speech is the supporter of the gods, of food, of every act of seeing and breathing; "they know it not, but yet they dwell beside me." The structure of the hymn, an "I am…" litany from a cosmic speaker, is the form of Geeta chapter 10 ("Of Vedas I am Sama… of mountains, Meru"), and the claim "through me alone all eat the food" resembles Geeta 9.4-9.10 ("I support all this universe… under my supervision Prakriti brings forth moving and unmoving"). The Vak hymn is, in effect, the Rig Veda's own first-person divine revelation.

### 8A.6 The Gambler's lament (10.34)

> "Sprung from tall trees on windy heights, these rollers transport me as they turn upon the table. Dearer to me the die that never slumbers than the deep draught of Mujavan's own Soma. She never vexed me nor was angry with me, but to my friends and me was ever gracious. For the die's sake, whose single point is final, mine own devoted wife I alienated. My wife holds me aloof, her mother hates me: the wretched man finds none to give him comfort… Dice, verily, are armed with goads and driving-hooks, deceiving and tormenting, causing grievous woe. They give frail gifts and then destroy the man who wins… Cast on the board, like lumps of magic charcoal, though cold themselves they burn the heart to ashes… The gambler's wife is left forlorn and wretched: the mother mourns the son who wanders homeless."

The hymn is a first-person confession of addiction, with a bitter, humane clarity. It ends with advice to give up the dice and plough one's field. It is a good corrective to any idea that the Vedic poets only wrote about gods: they also looked hard at human weakness. The same note of "desire that drives a man against his own will" is in Geeta 3.36 ("By what is a man impelled to commit sin, as if by force, even against his will?" The answer: desire and anger).

### 8A.7 Liberality (10.117): the ethic of giving

> "The Gods have not ordained hunger to be our death: even to the well-fed man comes death in varied shape. The riches of the liberal never waste away, while he who will not give finds none to comfort him… No friend is he who to his friend and comrade who comes imploring food, will offer nothing. Let him depart, no home is that to rest in… Let the rich satisfy the poor implorer, and bend his eye upon a longer pathway. Riches come now to one, now to another, and like the wheels of cars are ever rolling. The foolish man wins food with fruitless labour: that food, I speak the truth, shall be his ruin. He feeds no trusty friend, no man to love him. All guilt is he who eats with no partaker."

This is the clearest early statement of the ethics of sharing. "He who eats with no partaker" is guilty, and the Geeta repeats it: "Those who cook food only for themselves eat sin" (3.13), which closes the section on sacrifice in chapter 3. The image of riches as chariot wheels, "now to one, now to another," also anticipates the Geeta's idea that fortune turns, so one should not cling.

### 8A.8 The Frogs (7.103): a joke with a point

> "They who lay quiet for a year, the Brahmans who fulfil their vows, the Frogs have lifted up their voice, the voice Parjanya hath inspired… The music of the Frogs comes forth in concert like the cows lowing with their calves beside them."

A humorous hymn in which the frogs, croaking when the first monsoon rains arrive, are compared to priests chanting after a year of silence. Whatever else it is, it shows that the poets were willing to smile at their own rituals, and that the rainy season was the pulse of the whole religion. The Geeta's "beings come from food, food from rain, rain from sacrifice" (3.14) is the monsoon cycle in philosophical language.

### 8A.9 What these hymns teach about the Rig Veda

Taken together, these hymns show five things about the Rig Veda that the bare labels "polytheistic" or "ritual" hide.

1. It has a **moral interior** (Varuna's hymns, Liberality, the Gambler): the poet confesses, reflects, and gives advice.
2. It has an **aesthetic of nature** (the Dawn, the Frogs, the Rivers): the world is beautiful before it is useful.
3. It has a **cosmic myth of struggle and release** (Indra and Vritra): the waters must be set free.
4. It has an **aspiration beyond death** (Soma 9.113, the Tryambaka verse): people already wanted immortality, light and freedom, not just cattle.
5. It has a **first-person voice of the divine** (Vak, 10.125), which the Geeta will take over and make the voice of the whole book.

## 9. The Yajur Veda, the Sama Veda, and the Atharva Veda

### 9.1 The Yajur Veda: the book of the ritual act

**What it is.** The Yajur Veda is the collection of *yajus*, the short prose or verse formulas that the *adhvaryu* priest mutters while performing the physical acts of the sacrifice: measuring the ground, cutting the sacred grass, pouring the offering, handling the vessels. The Rig Veda is for singing the gods' praise; the Yajur Veda is the working manual for the rite.

**Two recensions.** The tradition has two great branches. The **White (Shukla) Yajur Veda** keeps the formulas apart from the explanatory prose, and its text is called the Vajasaneyi Samhita. The **Black (Krishna) Yajur Veda** mixes formulas and explanatory prose in the same text, and its best-known version is the **Taittiriya Samhita**. The 2002 compilation says exactly this at the start of its Yajur Veda section. Keith's translation in your Book 1 is of the Taittiriya Samhita (the "Veda of the Black Yajus School").

**Structure (Keith, Book 1).** Seven *kandas* (books), each divided into *prapathakas* (lessons), each in numbered sections, cited in the style "i. 1. 1," "iv. 5. 1," "vii. 4. 12." (Book 2 says six kandhas, an inconsistency in the compilation; Keith's seven is the standard count for this Samhita.) The sections are headed by the rite each covers. In Kanda 1 and 2 are the "New and Full Moon sacrifices," the "Soma Sacrifice," "The Victim for Agni and Soma," "The Soma Cups." Kanda 4 contains the famous "Offerings to Rudra." Kanda 5 and 6 treat the building of the fire altar and the Soma pressing. Kanda 7 treats the horse sacrifice (*ashvamedha*), the "Gavam Ayana" (a year-long cow rite), and other great rites.

**What it reads like.** The first lines show it at once:

> "For food thee, for strength thee! Ye are winds, ye are approachers. Let the god Savitar impel you to the most excellent offering. O invincible ones, swell with the share for the gods, full of strength, of milk, rich in offspring, free from sickness, from disease. Let no thief, no evil worker, have control over you. Let Rudra's dart avoid you."

The speaker is addressing the *calves*, the actual calves that are to be separated from their mothers so that the milk can be gathered for the offering. The formulas are spoken to objects and to the priest's own action: to the twig, to the strew of sacred grass, to the knife, to the pot. Each one blesses, names, and sanctifies a physical act. This is why the Yajur Veda can feel like a stage manual with a liturgy attached.

**Why bother with it?** Three reasons.

1. **It shows the Vedic worldview in action.** To the ritualist, getting the formula and the movement exactly right is not superstition but the *mechanism* by which the cosmos is sustained. The word *karma* in its original Vedic sense means precisely this: ritual action.
2. **It is the background for the Geeta's key move.** When the Geeta (3.9) says "the world is bound by action other than action done as sacrifice (*yajnartha*)," it is redefining *yajna*, the central category of the Yajur Veda, as any selfless work done for the whole. The Geeta's *karma yoga* is the Yajur Veda's ritual karma, internalized and universalized.
3. **It contains the Rudra litany.** Kanda 4, prapathaka 5, is the **Offerings to Rudra** (the "Shatarudriya," the hundred-fold praise of Rudra, also called the *Namakam*). It begins:

> "Homage to thy wrath, O Rudra, to thine arrow homage also; homage to thy bow, and homage to thine arms. With thy most kindly arrow, and kindly bow, with thy kindly missile, be gentle to us, O Rudra. That body of thine, O Rudra, which is kindly, not dread, with auspicious look, with that body, most potent to heal, O haunter of the mountains, do thou look on us."

Notice the psychology: the poet bows to the *terrible* power and begs that it show its *kind* face. The same duality is present in the Geeta's eleventh chapter, where the vision of the Universal Form is both wonderful and terrifying, and Arjuna begs Krishna to return to his gentle human form. The litany goes on to honour Rudra in every guise of the world, from the highest to the lowest: the lord of thieves and the lord of the forest, of the horse and the carpenter. The point of the litany is that the divine includes everything, including the fierce.

**Takeaway for the Yajur Veda:** it is not a book of philosophy. It teaches that *how* an act is done matters, that every act can be sacrificial, and that the sacred is present in matter, tools and time. When you read it with the Geeta, treat it as the thing the Geeta is *answering*.

### 9.2 The Sama Veda: the book of the song

**What it is.** The Samaveda, in Griffith's preface (dated Kotagiri, Nilgiri, 25 May 1893, in your Book 1), is "the Veda of Holy Songs, third in the usual order of enumeration of the three Vedas," ranking "next in sanctity and liturgical importance to the Rigveda." It is "made up of hymns, portions of hymns, and detached verses, taken mainly from the Rigveda, transposed and re-arranged, without reference to their original order, to suit the religious ceremonies in which they were to be employed," chiefly for the **Udgatar** priests, in the Soma sacrifices.

The text we have is the *words only*. The actual chants (*samans*) involve prolongation, repetition, insertion of syllables, modulations, rests, and other modifications, which are recorded in separate song-books called *Ganas*. Griffith lists two for Part I, the *Gramageyagana* (village or congregational song-book) and the *Aranyagana* (forest song-book), and two for Part II, the *Uhagana* and *Uhyagana*. The tradition has three recensions: the Kauthuma (current in Gujarat), the Jaiminiya (in the Carnatic), and the Ranayaniya (in Maharashtra). Griffith followed Benfey's text of the Ranayaniya recension. He notes that nothing is known of the date or compiler.

**Structure.**

- **Part I** (the *Purvarchika*, "first collection"): six books, each of two chapters, arranged in "decades" of about ten verses. Book 1 opens with Agni: "Come, Agni, praised with song, to feast and sacrificial offering: sit as Hotar on the holy grass!" The content is mostly hymns to Agni, then Indra, then Soma. The compilation reports about 650 mantras here (with a note questioning the number).
- **Part II** (the *Uttararchika*, "later collection"): nine books, each of three chapters. This part is "less disjointed than Part I," generally arranged in **triplets** (groups of three verses), whose first verse is often repeated from Part I. It reports about 1,225 mantras.
- Total: the compilation gives 1,875 mantras, with a bracketed "[1,800?]," which shows the editors were unsure. The standard figure is 1,875 and the Sama Veda has only a few dozen verses that are not also in the Rig Veda.

**Why it matters.** It teaches that **sound itself is sacred**: that the same words, sung rather than spoken, produce a different effect on both singer and world. It is the ancestor of the Indian musical tradition (the seven notes are first named in Sama chanting). In the Geeta, Krishna says "Among the Vedas I am the Sama Veda" (10.22), "among the Samans I am the Brihat Saman" (10.35), and "Of the sacred utterances I am the one syllable Om" (10.25, 17.24). The Geeta's praise of the Sama Veda is a statement that, of the four, the most musical and least verbal is the closest to the divine.

### 9.3 The Atharva Veda: the book of life, healing, and mystery

**What it is.** The Atharva Veda is the odd one out. It is called the Veda of the *Atharvans* and *Angirases*, the fire-priest clans, and it was long treated as lower in status than the other three, because it deals with everyday concerns. It is a different world from the high ritual of the Soma sacrifice: healing, family, kingship, farming, spells, antidotes, curses, and also some of the most profound speculative hymns in the whole Vedic corpus.

**Size.** The 2002 compilation says it "contains at least 20 Kandhas," that the traditional text has over 6,000 hymns in the Shaunakiya recension and another 3,500 in the Paippalada recension, and it adds a tally of the five Kalpas (supplementary ritual manuals) to speculate about as many as 12,300. These figures go well beyond the standard tradition, which counts about 730 hymns and about 6,000 verses in the Shaunakiya Samhita. The 2002 draft only gives actual text for a subset (it lists 1,320 hymns in its planned allotment, with hymn counts per kandha: 112, 91, 40, 105, 40, 142, 139, 20, 29, 16, and so on). Take its large numbers as aspirational.

**Bloomfield's selection (Book 1).** Bloomfield's volume is a *selection*, arranged by *topic* rather than by the order of the original books, with an excerpt from his introduction. Its ten headed categories, which I mapped from the contents and read in part, run as follows.

- **I. Charms to cure diseases and possession by demons of disease** (*bhaishajyani*): fever (*takman*), consumption, jaundice, wounds, snakebite, worms, poison. The charms combine plant medicine with incantation.
- **II. Prayers for long life and health** (*ayushyani*).
- **III. Charms pertaining to women** (*strikarmani*): love, marriage, conception, safe birth, rivalry between co-wives.
- **Charms pertaining to royalty** (*rajakarmani*): the consecration of a king, military prayers, protection of the realm.
- **VI. Charms to secure harmony and influence in the assembly** (*sammanasyani*): reconciliation, concord in the household, success in the council.
- **VII. Charms for prosperity in house, field, cattle, business, gambling:** a "prayer at the building of a house" ("Right here do I erect a firm house; may it stand upon a good foundation, dripping with ghee"), prayers for stray calves, for success at dice, for exorcising serpents, prayers to Bhava and Sharva (a name of Rudra) for protection, a charm for finding lost property, and a hymn for the weather-prophet.
- **VIII. Charms in expiation of sin and defilement:** prayers against "mental delinquency," exorcism of evil dreams, expiation for ritual mistakes, and, among others, a prayer for heaven after the remission of sins.
- **IX. Prayers and imprecations in the interest of the Brahmans:** the priestly class's rights, gifts (*dakshina*), the preparation of the ritual porridge (*brahmaudana*).
- **X. Cosmogonic and theosophic hymns:** the intellectual jewels of the book.

The contrast between category I and category X is the whole Atharva Veda in miniature: from a spell against a fever to a poem about Time that carries the universe.

### 9.4 Three Atharva hymns that matter for the Geeta

I read these three in full.

**(a) The Hymn to Earth (12.1; Bloomfield's "Hymn to goddess Earth").** A long poem, 63 verses in the full version, in praise of the Earth as mother, protector and provider. It begins by naming what holds her up:

> "Truth, greatness, universal order (rita), strength, consecration, creative fervour (tapas), spiritual exaltation (brahma), the sacrifice, support the earth. May this earth, the mistress of that which was and shall be, prepare for us a broad domain!"

The hymn goes on to praise her mountains, plains, forests, rivers, her food and the tribes of men who arose on her, her fire, her paths, and it ends in intimate address: "The earth is the mother, and I the son of the earth; Parjanya is the father; he, too, shall save us!" (12.12.) It is an ecological hymn, perhaps the earliest in world literature. It also reads as the Atharva Veda's counterweight to the Rig Veda's sacrifice-first view: here the Earth is not a ritual object but the living ground.

For the Geeta: **Prakriti** (the Geeta's name for nature or matter) is a field and a womb, "the womb in which I place the seed" (14.3-4). The Hymn to Earth belongs to the same maternal view.

**(b) The Hymns to Time (19.53 and 19.54; Bloomfield's "Prayer to Kala").** These are the most remarkable pieces in the book.

> "Time, the steed, runs with seven reins (rays), thousand-eyed, ageless, rich in seed. The seers, thinking holy thoughts, mount him; all the beings (worlds) are his wheels. With seven wheels does this Time ride, seven naves has he, immortality is his axle. He carries hither all these beings (worlds). Time, the first god, now hastens onward."
> "Time begot yonder heaven, Time also (begot) these earths. That which was, and that which shall be, urged forth by Time, spreads out."
> "In Time mind is fixed, in Time breath, in Time names; when Time has arrived all these creatures rejoice. In Time tapas is fixed; in Time the highest (being) is fixed; in Time brahma is fixed; Time is the lord of everything; he was the father of Pragapati."

Time here is the creator, sustainer, and carrier of all, older than the creator-god Prajapati himself. A few lines later: "Being their father, he became their son; there is, verily, no other force, higher than he." This hymn is the closest Vedic ancestor of one of the most startling lines in the Geeta, when Krishna in the eleventh chapter says, "I am Time (*kala*), the destroyer of the worlds, grown vast, engaged here in drawing in the worlds" (11.32). I discuss it further in Part C.

**(c) The hymn to Prana (11.4), the Breath.** "Reverence to Prana, to whom all this universe is subject, who has become the lord of the all, on whom the all is supported!" The hymn shows breath as rain, thunder, the life of plants, and the supreme spirit. It corresponds to the Upanishadic view that breath is the thread joining all, and the Geeta's 15.14 ("I enter the bodies of beings as the digestive fire and, joined with the in-breath and out-breath, digest the four kinds of food").

**Other cosmogonic hymns in Bloomfield's tenth group** that I mapped from headings: the Hymn to Rohita (the Sun as supreme, a prayer for sovereign power); the glorification of the sun as the primeval **Brahmacharin** (a student of sacred knowledge, 11.5: "The Brahmakarin moves inciting both hemispheres of the world; in him the gods are harmonised"); the hymn to **Kama** (Desire, 9.2, here treated mostly as a power to be invoked against enemies and ill dreams, so an entirely different tone from the Rig Veda's "Desire, primal seed of Spirit"); the apotheosis of the **Ukkhishta** (the "remainder" of the sacrifice, from which everything is born); and the hymn to the **honey-whip of the Ashvins**. I did not read all of these closely.

## 10. Cross-cutting themes in the Vedas

These are the ideas that run across all four collections.

### 10.1 Rita: the order of things

*Rita* is the principle that the universe is not a heap but a lawful whole. The sun rises because Rita holds it. The rivers flow because Rita holds them. A promise must be kept because Rita demands it. When the Atharva Veda's Hymn to Earth begins with "Truth, greatness, universal order (rita), strength…" it lists rita as one of the supports of the earth. Rita is the ancestor of **dharma**, which comes from the root *dhri*, "to hold or support." When Osho begins the Geeta by calling Kurukshetra the "field of dharma" and then complaining that when dharma's field has to be fought over, dharma itself is nearly lost, he is playing on exactly this idea: dharma is what holds, and the battle is a sign of its failure.

### 10.2 Yajna: the sacrifice as the cosmic exchange

In the Vedas, the sacrifice is not a gesture of loss. It is the metabolism of the cosmos: you give to fire, fire gives to gods, gods give rain, rain gives food. The Purusha hymn says the cosmos itself began in a sacrifice. This is a powerful idea: the universe is a gift-circuit. The Geeta endorses the circuit (3.10-16) but rewrites the content of "the gift": it is *work done without clinging to the fruit*.

### 10.3 Tapas: heat, effort, creative fervour

*Tapas* literally "heat" appears everywhere. In the Nasadiya hymn the One is born "by the great power of Warmth." In the Atharva Veda's Hymn to Earth, tapas is one of her supports. It is the generative energy behind creation and the discipline by which a seer generates inner power. The Geeta's chapter 17 gives its own analysis of tapas of body, speech and mind, sorted by the three qualities.

### 10.4 The One and the many

The Vedas have a progression: many gods (Agni, Indra, Varuna), then "they call it Indra, Mitra, Varuna, Agni" (1.164.46), then a single Lord of creatures (Prajapati, Hiranyagarbha), then a single Man (Purusha), then a single principle (Time, Breath, the Support, the One that breathed without breath). The development *within the Veda itself* of the One-behind-the-many is what makes the Geeta's synthesis possible: Krishna can say "I am the Self seated in the heart of all beings" (10.20), "Whatever deity a devotee worships with faith, I make that faith steady" (7.21), and "even those who worship other gods with faith, worship only me, though not in the proper way" (9.23).

### 10.5 Death and what is beyond it

From the Tryambaka verse's cucumber on the vine to the funeral hymns to Yama in the tenth book, the Vedas are preoccupied with death and what follows. The earliest hymns hope for long life (*shatam jivema sharadah*, "may we live a hundred autumns") and for a place among the "fathers" in heaven. The later layers hope for something deeper: release from death altogether. The Geeta's central teaching on the deathless self (2.20, "He is not born, nor does he ever die… unborn, eternal, everlasting, ancient") is the philosophical resolution of that long Vedic quest.

### 10.6 Speech, sound and the sacred word

Vak (Speech) is a goddess in her own right. The riddle hymn says three-quarters of speech is hidden. The Sama Veda teaches that the same words sung have different power. The Yajur Veda insists on the exact formula. At the end of that line is **Om**, the one-syllable essence that the Geeta names as "the sacred word in all the Vedas" (7.8, 8.13, 9.17, 17.24). This love of the *sacred utterance* explains why the whole Vedic tradition was preserved orally for millennia with extraordinary precision.

# PART B: THE GEETA, AS OSHO READS IT

## 11. The book and its setting

**What "Geeta Darshan" is.** *Geeta-Darshan* ("Vision of the Geeta") is a series of Hindi discourses by Osho (1931-1990) on the Bhagavad Geeta, given in the 1970s and later gathered into eight volumes (*Bhag 1* to *Bhag 8*), the edition you uploaded being the 2013 Osho Media International compilation in one PDF of 3,362 pages. The file's contents page lists the chapters and their starting pages: Chapters 1 and 2 together from page 3, Chapter 3 from page 303, Chapter 4 from 475, Chapter 5 from 759, Chapter 6 from 923, Chapter 7 from 1249, Chapter 8 from 1395, Chapter 9 from 1537, Chapter 10 from 1709, Chapter 11 from 1927, Chapter 12 from 2135, Chapter 13 from 2337, Chapter 14 from 2567, Chapter 15 from 2751, Chapter 16 from 2877, Chapter 17 from 3025, and Chapter 18 from 3261. The size of each chapter's coverage tells you something: Osho spends about 300 pages on Chapters 1 and 2 (the setup and the Sankhya teaching), about 170 on Chapter 3, 285 on Chapter 4, and then roughly 140 to 330 pages on each of the rest. Chapter 6 (meditation) gets about 325 pages, the largest single block after the first two.

**The format of each discourse.** Each discourse (*vachan*) begins with a few Sanskrit verses, a plain Hindi gloss of them, and then Osho speaks on the questions they raise, in his usual manner: framing a problem, telling a story (a Zen anecdote, a Sufi tale, a case from modern psychology), and returning to the verse. He does not comment sentence by sentence as a scholar would. He uses each verse as a springboard. A rough count of the Hindi word for "discourse" in the extracted text suggests about 677 occurrences across the book, and the number of discourses is in the hundreds.

**His general stance.** From what I read, three habits of mind recur. First, he treats the Geeta as a book about *the human condition*, not about a war long ago: Arjuna is every person at a crossroads. Second, he draws sharply on psychology (Freud, Adler, Pyrrho's scepticism, Polanyi) and on philosophy (Wittgenstein, Russell, Ayer) to explain why the Geeta's old words still bite. Third, he is hostile to *borrowed* answers. He keeps returning to the idea that a conclusion someone gives you is dead, and a conclusion you digest yourself is alive. That emphasis shapes everything he says about Krishna's method.

**The Geeta itself.** For readers who need the frame: the Bhagavad Geeta is a poem of 700 verses in 18 chapters, forming part of the Mahabharata's Bhishma Parva. The epic's context is a civil war between two branches of one family, the Pandavas and the Kauravas. Just before the first charge, the Pandava warrior Arjuna loses his nerve and lays down his bow, unwilling to kill kinsmen and teachers. His charioteer, Krishna, answers with eighteen chapters on action, knowledge, devotion, meditation and the nature of the self, and at the end Arjuna says "my delusion is gone, I will do as you say." The frame is the blind king Dhritarashtra asking his minister Sanjaya what is happening on the field, and Sanjaya narrating.

**A reading note.** In what follows, "Geeta" means the verses themselves, which I summarize from my own general knowledge of the text. "Osho" means what I read in the PDF, paraphrased in English. Each chapter note ends with a *read depth* tag that says how much of Osho's discourse I actually read, so you can judge how much weight to put on it.

## 12. Chapter by chapter

### Chapters 1 and 2: The crisis of Arjuna, and the Sankhya teaching
*(Osho: "The thoughtful Arjuna and the dilemma of righteousness in war", opening discourse; read depth: opening pages and a long passage on the senses and on firmness of mind.)*

**The Geeta.** Chapter 1 is the *Arjuna-vishada-yoga*, "the yoga of Arjuna's dejection." Both armies are drawn up. Arjuna asks Krishna to drive the chariot between them and sees, on both sides, teachers, uncles, cousins and friends. His body trembles, his bow slips, his mouth dries, and he declares that he would rather be killed unresisting than win a kingdom at that price. Chapter 2, the *Sankhya-yoga*, is Krishna's first answer and the longest of the early chapters. It contains the teaching of the **immortal self** ("the wise grieve neither for the living nor for the dead," 2.11; "never was there a time when I did not exist," 2.12; "as a man casts off worn-out clothes and puts on new ones, so the embodied one casts off worn-out bodies," 2.22; "the self is neither born nor does it die," 2.20), the teaching of **duty without attachment to fruit** ("you have a right to action alone, never to its fruits," 2.47), and the portrait of the **sthitaprajna**, the one of steady wisdom (2.54-72), who is "undisturbed in sorrow, free from longing in pleasure, free from attachment, fear and anger."

**Osho's reading.**

*Dhritarashtra's blindness.* The very first verse of the Geeta is not spoken by Krishna but by the blind king: "In the field of dharma, the field of the Kurus, what did my sons and the sons of Pandu do, assembled and eager to fight?" Osho builds a whole discourse on this opening. The king is blind, but blindness of the eyes does not end desire; he says "if Surdas had thought of Dhritarashtra, he wouldn't have needed to put out his eyes." Desire lives in the mind, not in the eyes. A blind man still wants to see; a deaf man still wants to hear. All stories begin, Osho says, with the curiosity of a blind person. He also points out that the king's hundred sons, though sighted, inherited his *inner* blindness: "from the blind comes only blindness."

*The "field of dharma" that needs a battle.* Osho makes a sharp observation: the king calls Kurukshetra a "field of dharma," but "on the day dharma's own field has to be a place of battle, it has stopped being a field of dharma, and on the day one has to fight even in the field of dharma, the possibility of dharma surviving is over." It might have been dharma's field once; that day it was a place where people had assembled to cut each other down. This is the same note as in the Vedic idea of *rita* as that-which-holds, and it shows Osho reading the text as a diagnosis of a failing civilisation.

*The senses are innocent.* In his comment on the verses about the senses (the Geeta's third chapter contains the line that the person who restrains the organs of action but sits mentally dwelling on sense objects is "of deluded understanding, a hypocrite," 3.6), Osho launches into a long argument against suppression. The senses, he says, have no desires of their own; the mind does, and the senses follow like servants. The eye sees beauty because the mind asks it to. When we suppress the senses, the mind, deprived of its outer food, "goes mad and starts creating inside the things it wanted outside." If you fast all day, at night you are invited to a palace in your dream. He points to Freud's reaction to the ascetic traditions he met (people who whipped themselves, stayed awake to avoid dreams, starved the body so that it would have no strength), and says that Freud called all that "pathological," and that Krishna's single line (calling the suppressor *mudha*, deluded) opens Freud's knots *five thousand years earlier*. The senses are "completely innocent." The cure is not to fight them but to understand the mind that uses them.

*Doing and not-doing (akarma).* For the passage on *akarma* (non-action), Osho tells a Zen story. A Japanese emperor visits the master Bankei's monastery of 500 monks and is shown the dining hall, the meditation halls, the study halls. He keeps asking about the golden-spired temple in the middle. Each time the emperor asks, Bankei goes deaf and walks him on to another building. In the end the emperor, at the gate, nearly concludes he is mad or the master is. The temple at the centre is the thing that can't be shown because it is the *empty centre* around which all the doing arranges itself. Osho's point is that what must be found is "what stands behind all doing and yet remains outside it." One does not find the self by working harder at *doing*; one finds it by stepping back to see the one who is doing.

*Firmness and its counterfeit.* On the verses about the steady mind, Osho takes up Adler's psychology. What looks like firm resolve on the outside is often a mask, he says; "inside, the face is shaking every moment." The bravest-looking person is frightened inside; the "so-called knower" is trembling in ignorance. Adler's insight, as Osho tells it, is that people compensate: the more inferior one feels inside, the more superiority one stages outside. Politicians, he says, are always sufferers from inferiority complexes, because only someone who feels like "nothing" would run so hard to prove he is "something." So when Krishna asks for a *steady* intellect, it cannot be this staged firmness. It has to be a mind that no longer needs to hide its trembling.

**Why this section matters.** It sets the tone of the book: the Geeta is read as an inward text; the war is a metaphor for the human crisis, and the early teaching is "know what is deathless in you."

### Chapter 3: Karma Yoga, the discovery of one's own dharma
*(Osho: "The search for one's own dharma", chapter 3, first discourse; read depth: opening discourse.)*

**The Geeta.** Chapter 3 begins with Arjuna's complaint: "If you think knowledge is better than action, why do you urge me into this terrible action?" Krishna's reply is the foundation of *karma yoga*: no one can remain even for a moment without acting (3.5), because nature compels everyone. The point is to act without attachment (3.19). He then gives the logic of **sacrifice**: "Beings come from food, food from rain, rain from sacrifice (*yajna*), and sacrifice from action" (3.14); "the world is bound by action, other than action done as sacrifice" (3.9); and he says that even Krishna himself acts, though he has nothing to gain, "lest the worlds perish" (3.22-24). The chapter also contains the line that it is better to do one's own *svadharma* poorly than another's well (3.35), and ends on the problem of desire: "It is desire, it is anger, born of rajas" (3.37), which can only be conquered by knowing the self.

**Osho's reading.** Osho opens with a sharp question that frames the whole chapter: *is the truth of life obtained by action or by knowledge?* If action, then it is something you don't presently have, something you could earn and could lose again in sleep. A thing obtained by effort is *acquired*, not your nature. Truth cannot be of that kind. Truth is already given: it is the thing "that we have never lost, that we couldn't lose even if we tried; it is the life of our life." What we have lost is only its *remembrance*. Our eyes are shut, the light is there; the door is closed, the sun is there. "You don't have to go and *get* the sun; you only have to open the door."

He then sets up a large and important contrast. All human thought, he says, divides into two streams, **Sankhya** and **Yoga**. Even those who never heard those words (Aristotle, Socrates, Abraham, Ezekiel, Lao Tzu, Confucius) stand in one or the other. Sankhya says: truth can be known by knowing alone; nothing else need be done. Yoga says (Osho sketches this side more briefly in what I read) that one must do the work of transforming oneself. Arjuna, who has just heard the Sankhya view in Chapter 2, now raises exactly the right objection. If knowing is enough, why is Krishna pressing him into a "terrible" battle?

**Why this section matters.** It stages the central tension of the Geeta between *knowing* and *doing*, and sets up Krishna's resolution: a way of acting that is itself a form of knowing.

### Chapter 4: Knowledge and action, the eternal teaching
*(Osho: discourse on the timeless; read depth: opening discourse.)*

**The Geeta.** Chapter 4 opens with the verse that sets the Geeta in cosmic time: "I taught this imperishable yoga to Vivasvan (the Sun); Vivasvan told it to Manu; Manu told it to Ikshvaku" (4.1). The teaching was lost over time and Krishna is teaching it again. Then comes the doctrine of **avatara**: "Whenever dharma declines and adharma rises, I manifest myself" (4.7-8). The chapter explains that the wise see **inaction in action and action in inaction** (4.18), that a *jnani* acts without binding himself (4.19-23), that the sacrifice of knowledge is better than any material sacrifice (4.33), and that "nothing in this world purifies like knowledge" (4.38).

**Osho's reading.** Osho opens on the word *sanatana*, eternal. Truth is neither new nor old. What is new becomes old; what is old was once new; anything that goes from new to old goes from birth to death. Truth has neither birth nor death. So it can be neither new nor old: it is *sanatana*, outside time, "beyond time" (he uses the English phrase "beyond time"). Everything that begins begins inside time; therefore Krishna's saying "at the beginning of time I told this to the Sun" can only mean *outside time*.

Then Osho reads the Sun symbolically. Those who have gone deep into the inner life report one common experience: in the last depth only *light* remains and everything else disappears. When one enters the inner void, the ego dissolves, "only light remains." To say that Krishna told the Sun means he told *light* the first time. The first event of the world is light and the last event of the world is light; the first event of spiritual life is light and the last is light. The deepest experiences, the first and the final, are the same.

**Why this section matters.** It is a good example of how Osho uses a mythic detail to carry a mystical claim. It also links directly to the Vedic Savitar and Gayatri material in Part A: the Sun as the source of insight.

### Chapter 5: Renunciation and selfless action
*(Osho: "The declaration of renunciation (sannyas)"; read depth: opening discourse.)*

**The Geeta.** Arjuna is confused by Krishna's praise of both renunciation of action and *nishkama karma* (action without desire for results): "Tell me decisively which is better" (5.1). Krishna answers that both lead to the supreme goal, but that *karma yoga* is better than mere renunciation (5.2); the true sannyasi is one who "neither hates nor desires" (5.3). The wise see knowledge and action as one (5.4-5). The chapter ends with a meditation instruction in short form (5.27-28, controlling the breath and the gaze between the brows) and the promise of "peace" to those who know Krishna as "the friend of all beings" (5.29).

**Osho's reading.** Osho's discourse begins from Arjuna's request for a "decisive" answer. The human mind, he says, always wants someone else to decide. Making a decision is a pain; deliberating is a penance; to think for yourself is *tapasya*. We want the truth to come ready-made, without effort. But, Osho says, if you look at how Krishna has actually spoken, it was not an *order*. He has laid out the problems and illuminated every road, and left the final decision to Arjuna. "The greatest masters in the world always avoid giving disciples a conclusion," he says, because a conclusion that is lent is stale and dead. Borrowed decisions "become like flowers bought in the market and stuck in a vase, not flowers that have grown from the ground, breathing the wind and drinking the sun." And he adds a sharp comparison: even if food were available *pre-digested*, we would refuse to do the digesting, but on the day we are given digested food, "we will be men of the grave, not living men."

**Why this section matters.** It tells you how Osho thinks Krishna teaches: by opening choices, not by commanding conclusions.

### Chapter 6: Meditation, and Krishna's new sannyas
*(Osho: "Krishna's sannyas: a sannyas of celebration"; read depth: opening discourse.)*

**The Geeta.** The sixth chapter, the *Dhyana-yoga*, opens with the verse that "the one who does what ought to be done, not seeking its fruit, is a sannyasi and a yogi, not the one who has merely given up the (sacrificial) fire and rites" (6.1). It then teaches meditation in concrete steps: the seat, posture, the gaze, "let him lift himself by the self; let him not lower himself" (6.5), the equanimity of the yogi to heat, cold, praise and blame (6.7), the right measure in eating, sleeping and effort (6.16-17), the steady flame in a windless place as a picture of a concentrated mind (6.19), and the restless mind, which Krishna admits is "as hard to control as the wind" (6.34) but which can be controlled by practice and dispassion. It ends with the question of the yogi who fails, and Krishna's comfort that no good effort is ever lost (6.40), and a final verse that the yogi is greater than the ascetic, the scholar, and the ritualist (6.46).

**Osho's reading.** Here Osho states the claim that "with Krishna a new concept of sannyas was born on earth." Sannyas had always been *world-negating*: the flight from life, from home, from action, a "life-negative" posture. Krishna gives it a new dimension, an "inner transformation" over an outer renunciation. Arjuna's own idea of sannyas was precisely the old one: leave it all and go. Krishna says the real sannyasi is one who acts without craving the fruit. Osho notes two parts of sannyas: its outer form and its "inner soul"; the old kind stressed the outer form, Krishna stresses the *inner transformation*. And he adds a barbed remark: quitting work is easy, "even the lazy sit down and quit," so it is no surprise that the old concept attracted lazy people. The discourse title as it appears in the file also calls Krishna's sannyas "utsavpurna," full of festival, joyful.

**Why this section matters.** It is the best single summary of the Geeta's main reform: renunciation moved from the *outside* of life to the *inside*.

### Chapter 7: Knowledge and realization, undivided faith
*(Osho: "Undivided devotion", *ananya nishtha*; read depth: opening pages.)*

**The Geeta.** Chapter 7, *Jnana-vijnana-yoga*, opens a new movement: Krishna promises to teach the knowledge "with realization" (7.2). He describes his **two natures**, the lower *prakriti* in eight forms (earth, water, fire, air, ether, mind, intellect, ego) and the higher nature, the life-principle that sustains the universe (7.4-5), and says "all this is strung on me like rows of gems on a thread" (7.7). He then lists how he is present in things: "I am the taste in water… the light in the sun and moon… the sacred syllable Om in all the Vedas" (7.8). He teaches that his divine *maya* (made of the three gunas) is hard to cross, but those who take refuge in him cross it (7.14), and that four kinds of people worship him: the distressed, the seeker of knowledge, the seeker of wealth, the man of wisdom, "of whom the wise man, constantly steadfast and devoted to the One, is the best" (7.16-17). The chapter ends with "Vasudeva is all" (7.19).

**Osho's reading.** Osho opens with a definition of dharma: "Fundamentally, dharma is the name of a loving trust (*nishtha*) toward life." There are two attitudes towards life, he says: *refusal* and *acceptance*. The more ego one has inside, the more one refuses life, and the more humility one has, the more one accepts it. He then, in what I read, develops the idea of *love as prayer* and the dissolution of distance. Distance between lover and beloved remains as long as the other is limited; only in prayer does it break, and only with someone without limit. There is a "mighty strange thing": when the other vanishes, the "I" vanishes too, because "I" and "thou" are two sides of one coin; throw one away and the other disappears. This is why Vedanta uses the word **advaita**, *non-dual*: it doesn't say "one remains," it says "two do not remain," because "one" can exist only in contrast with an other.

**Why this section matters.** It shows Osho giving a precise reason why Vedanta's most characteristic word is a negation. It also anticipates the later chapters on devotion and the universal form.

### Chapter 8: The imperishable Brahman, the moment of death
*(Osho: discourse opens on the first verses; read depth: verse text and opening only; I did not read enough of the discourse to attribute any argument.)*

**The Geeta.** Chapter 8, *Akshara-brahma-yoga*, begins with Arjuna's seven questions: What is Brahman? What is *adhyatma*? What is karma? What is *adhibhuta*? What is *adhidaiva*? Who is *adhiyajna* and how is he in the body? How is he known at the time of death? (8.1-2). Krishna defines each (8.3-4): Brahman is the imperishable supreme; *adhyatma* is one's own nature; karma is the creative force that causes beings to exist; *adhibhuta* is the perishable; *adhidaiva* is the cosmic person; *adhiyajna* is Krishna himself in the body. Then the famous statement: "Whoever at the end of life, leaving the body, goes forth remembering me alone, attains my being; of this there is no doubt" (8.5) and "whatever state of being a man remembers when he leaves the body, to that he goes" (8.6). The chapter describes the **Day and Night of Brahma** (a day of a thousand yugas, 8.17), the cycle of creation and dissolution, the way of light and way of darkness at death (8.24-26), and ends by saying the yogi who knows this "transcends all the fruits of the Vedas, sacrifice, austerity and charity, and reaches the supreme, primal abode" (8.28).

**Osho's reading (not sampled in depth).** The title of the discourse in the file is garbled in extraction, and I read only the verses and the first lines. I therefore don't attribute an argument to Osho here.

### Chapter 9: The royal knowledge, the royal secret
*(Osho: "The sprouting of faith"; read depth: opening discourse.)*

**The Geeta.** Chapter 9, *Raja-vidya-raja-guhya-yoga*, is Krishna's most intimate teaching: "the most secret knowledge, the king of sciences, the king of secrets, the purifier, directly perceptible, in accord with dharma, easy to practise, imperishable" (9.1-2). He says: "All this world is pervaded by me in my unmanifest form; all beings abide in me, but I do not abide in them" (9.4), and "The Lord's *yoga* is wondrous: my self sustains beings but doesn't depend on them" (9.5). He offers the famous verse on devotion: "Whoever offers me with love a leaf, a flower, a fruit, or water, I accept that offering of the loving heart" (9.26). He promises "To those who worship me alone, thinking of nothing else, I bring what they lack and preserve what they have" (9.22), and says even a person of evil conduct who worships him with undivided heart should be considered righteous (9.30-31). He also describes the worshippers of the Vedic gods, "knowers of the three Vedas who drink the Soma" (9.20-21), who reach the heaven of Indra, enjoy its pleasures, and when their merit is exhausted return to the mortal world, in contrast to those who come to him.

**Osho's reading.** He opens by distinguishing the *negative* from the *positive* way of seeing life. You can look at life so that nothing is seen but matter, or so that nothing is seen but God. Those who say "life is only matter" are saying only that their way of looking is negative; those who say "life is not matter" are saying that their way of looking is affirmative. "The world appears just as our eye is." He then draws on the philosopher-scientist Michael Polanyi, whose book *Personal Knowledge* is, Osho says, one of the century's most important, to argue against the assumption that science observes from a position of no standpoint. In the last three hundred years of scientific work, he says, the foundation of science itself has been shaken; scientists now say there is no way to see anything except from a standpoint. Osho's point: "faith" (*shraddha*) is not credulity but the attitude that makes some truths visible at all.

**Why this section matters.** It is the best example in my sample of Osho's method of addressing a contemporary sceptic in the middle of an ancient text.

### Chapter 10: The Vibhutis, the splendours
*(Osho: "The unknowable mystery of life"; read depth: opening pages.)*

**The Geeta.** Chapter 10, *Vibhuti-yoga*, is a catalogue of how the divine appears in the world. After a prelude ("Neither the hosts of gods nor the great sages know my origin, for I am the source of all of them," 10.2), Krishna lists the "manifestations" (*vibhutis*): "Of the Adityas I am Vishnu; of lights, the radiant Sun; of the Maruts, Marichi; of the stars, the Moon" (10.21); "Of the Vedas I am the Sama Veda; of the gods, Indra; of the senses, the mind" (10.22); "Of the Rudras I am Shankara" (10.23); "Of mountains I am Meru" (10.23); "Of sacrifices I am the sacrifice of silent repetition; of immovables, the Himalaya" (10.25); "Of trees, the sacred fig; of sages, Narada" (10.26); "Of weapons I am the thunderbolt; of cows, the wish-fulfilling cow" (10.28); "Of purifiers I am the wind; of warriors, Rama; of fishes, the shark; of rivers, the Ganges" (10.31); "Of letters I am the letter A; of compounds, the dvandva; I am inexhaustible Time" (10.33); "Of secrets I am silence; of the knowers, the knowledge" (10.38); and then the summary: "Whatever being is glorious, prosperous or powerful, know that to arise from a fragment of my splendour… I pervade this entire universe with a single fragment of myself, and remain" (10.41-42).

**Osho's reading.** Osho opens by taking up what the Geeta calls the "supreme statement" (*parama vachana*). A supreme statement, he says, is one that goes beyond our ordinary labels of "true" and "false." He then engages directly with the logical positivists: Bertrand Russell, Ludwig Wittgenstein and A.J. Ayer have called statements like "God exists" *nonsense*, "meaningless," because they can be neither verified nor refuted. Ayer worked hard to prove that some statements are unverifiable and therefore empty. Osho does not wave this away. He then proposes that life itself is a **mystery**, not a *puzzle*: a puzzle is a mathematical problem that yields to effort; a mystery is the very nature of life. "Life is a mystery."

**Why this section matters.** It is the right framework for reading chapter 10. A list of "I am this, I am that" is not a ranking of things; it is a way of pointing at something that cannot be solved like a riddle, only approached.

### Chapter 11: The Universal Form
*(Osho: "The preparation for meeting the Vast"; read depth: opening pages.)*

**The Geeta.** Chapter 11, *Vishvarupa-darshana-yoga*, is the poetic summit of the Geeta. Arjuna asks to *see* the cosmic form Krishna has described, and Krishna says he cannot see it with his ordinary eye; he gives Arjuna "a divine eye" (11.8). What Arjuna sees is staggering: Krishna with "many mouths and eyes, many wondrous sights, many divine ornaments… the whole universe in one place, divided in many ways, in the body of the God of gods" (11.10-13). Its brilliance is "as if a thousand suns rose at once in the sky" (11.12; this is the verse J. Robert Oppenheimer is said to have recalled at the Trinity test). The vision then turns terrible: Arjuna sees the armies rushing into the mouths of the form "like moths into a flame," and the Lord says: "I am Time (*kala*), the destroyer of worlds, grown vast and engaged here in drawing in the worlds. Even without you, none of those warriors arrayed will live" (11.32). Arjuna trembles, begs to see again Krishna's gentle human form, which is restored (11.45-51). The chapter ends with the statement that only by exclusive devotion can this form be known and entered (11.54).

**Osho's reading.** Osho opens on a paradox of spiritual life: *the Lord is not obtained without effort*, and yet *when he is obtained, the one who obtains him feels it was not the fruit of his effort but the Lord's grace*. The finder knows that what he did is worth nothing and what he has found is beyond all value; he sees it is *prasad*, grace, favour. But a person who has not yet found, and who concludes "since it comes by grace, I need do nothing," will never receive it. So what is the role of effort? Osho gives a simple image: a lake can't produce the rain, but it can make itself ready to receive it. Human effort does not make God appear, but it makes the person *capable* of receiving the downpour of grace.

**Why this section matters.** It resolves an old question (effort versus grace) in a sentence, and it prepares the reader for the single most dramatic scene of the Geeta.

### Chapter 12: The path of devotion
*(Osho: "The door of love, entering devotion"; read depth: opening pages.)*

**The Geeta.** Chapter 12, *Bhakti-yoga*, opens with Arjuna's question: those who worship you with devotion as a personal God, and those who worship the imperishable unmanifest, "which of these has the better knowledge of yoga?" (12.1). Krishna answers that those who fix their minds on him with supreme faith are "most perfect in yoga," while those who worship the unmanifest also reach him, but "the path is hard for the embodied" (12.2-5). He then gives a graded programme (12.8-12): fix your mind on me; if you cannot, practise; if you cannot, do all work for me; if you cannot, renounce the fruit. The second half of the chapter (12.13-20) is a portrait of the person dear to him: "free from hatred, friendly and compassionate, free from the sense of 'mine' and 'I,' equal in pain and pleasure, forgiving, contented, self-controlled, of firm resolve, with mind and intellect given to me."

**Osho's reading.** Osho begins by distinguishing two worlds. One is the world the intellect can grasp; here mathematics and logic work, and arguments can be made and conclusions drawn. The other breaks logic's rules; the intellect finds the door shut, and only the *heart* can enter. In that world "to be blind is to have eyes, and to be a fool is to be wise." Devotion is the entry into that second world, and it demands staking oneself entirely: "where we fail, truth does not necessarily fail." He then tells a Sufi story about the mystic Bayazid. A bird flies into a closed room through a window and beats itself against walls, doors and ceiling, growing more frantic, ignoring the open window by which it came in. Bayazid sits meditating and thinks: how can one explain to this bird that *the way that brought you in also takes you out*? Only the direction changes; the door is the same. The bird was not stupid; it simply fixated on one idea of how it had entered. So we, too, he implies, flail at everything but the window.

**Why this section matters.** It gives two ideas that stay with you: devotion belongs to the heart's world, and the way out is not a new door but the one you came in by.

### Chapter 13: The field and the knower of the field
*(Osho: "The way out of suffering: dissolving identification"; read depth: opening discourse.)*

**The Geeta.** Chapter 13, *Kshetra-kshetrajna-vibhaga-yoga*, begins with Krishna's definition: "This body, O Arjuna, is called the *kshetra*, the field; he who knows it is called the *kshetrajna*, the knower of the field" (13.1). "Know me also as the knower of the field in all fields" (13.2). The chapter lists the qualities that constitute knowledge, *jnana* (humility, non-violence, patience, uprightness, service of the teacher, purity, steadiness, self-control, detachment, absence of ego; 13.7-11), describes the *Brahman* "beginningless, the Supreme, said to be neither being nor non-being" (13.12), "with hands and feet everywhere, eyes, heads and faces everywhere" (13.13), the light of lights, and then explains **Prakriti** and **Purusha**: Prakriti is the cause of action, Purusha the experiencer of pleasure and pain (13.20-21). The Supreme Self "dwelling in the body is the witness (*upadrashta*), the permitter, the supporter, the experiencer, the great Lord" (13.22). The chapter ends with the image of the self as the sun that illumines the entire world (13.33) and "those who see with the eye of knowledge the difference between the field and the knower of the field reach the Supreme" (13.34).

**Osho's reading.** Osho begins with something he says he finds every day: "From morning to evening I meet an uncountable number of sufferers, and there is one thing I look for: a sufferer whose suffering has some *other* cause. So far I haven't found one." Whatever the form of suffering, the cause is always the self; the responsibility for it is always one's own. Suffering seems to come from outside, and people blame circumstances, people, the world; but all the apparent causes are false until the *real* cause is seen. If the diagnosis is wrong, the treatment cannot work. The chapter's title (as the file's page header gives it) names the cure: the *dissolving of identification*, of taking oneself to be the field.

**Why this section matters.** It reads the *kshetra/kshetrajna* distinction as practical psychology: the self-diagnosis is already half the cure.

### Chapter 14: The three Gunas
*(Osho: "Desire is the world, desirelessness is the supreme perfection"; read depth: opening pages.)*

**The Geeta.** Chapter 14, *Gunatraya-vibhaga-yoga*, is Krishna's exposition of **sattva, rajas and tamas**, the three qualities of Prakriti. Sattva is luminous and brings attachment to happiness and knowledge; rajas is passion, bringing attachment to action; tamas is born of ignorance and deludes all beings through heedlessness, laziness and sleep (14.5-9). When sattva prevails, the light of knowledge shines in all the gates of the body (14.11); when rajas prevails come greed, restlessness and craving (14.12); when tamas prevails come darkness, inertia and delusion (14.13). What one is at the time of death decides where one goes next (14.14-15). The one who "goes beyond the three gunas" (*gunatita*), seeing no one but the guna as the agent, is freed from birth, death, old age and sorrow (14.19-20). Arjuna asks for the marks of such a person, and Krishna describes someone who "does not hate light, activity or delusion when present, nor long for them when absent" (14.22), who is "the same in pleasure and pain, self-abiding, to whom a clod, stone and gold are alike" (14.24).

**Osho's reading.** He opens with a story of the Greek sceptic **Pyrrho**. Pyrrho, famous for the extreme doubt he lived out, is walking home one rainy evening and sees his own dog stuck in a ditch, sunk up to its neck in mud. He stops at the edge and thinks: should I pull the dog out or not? Pyrrho's conviction was that one should not act until the *consequences* are fully certain. Would saving the dog bring good or bad? What would the dog do in life afterwards, and would that be good or bad? Until he knew, he would not move. Osho uses Pyrrho to show the paralysis of the mind that wants certainty before acting; and uses it to introduce the Geeta's insistence that we are *driven* by the gunas whether we want to or not, so we cannot sit still waiting for certainty.

**Why this section matters.** It dramatizes why the Geeta thinks a person who tries to *wait for the perfect action* has not escaped the gunas but is simply caught in tamas dressed up as prudence.

### Chapter 15: The Supreme Person, the inverted tree
*(Osho: "The return to the original source"; read depth: opening discourse in some detail.)*

**The Geeta.** Chapter 15, *Purushottama-yoga*, opens with an image: "They speak of an eternal *ashvattha* (peepul, sacred fig) tree with roots above and branches below, whose leaves are the Vedas; he who knows it knows the Vedas" (15.1). Its branches spread above and below, nourished by the gunas, with sense-objects as buds; its roots below bind to action in the world of men (15.2). The remedy is to cut this tree "with the strong sword of detachment" (15.3) and "seek that path from which those who go do not return" (15.4). Krishna then describes the light of the sun, moon and fire as his light (15.12), says "I enter the earth and support beings by my power; I become the moon, Soma, and nourish the plants" (15.13), "I become the digestive fire in the body" (15.14), and "I am seated in the heart of all, from me are memory, knowledge and forgetting; I am to be known by all the Vedas; I am the author of Vedanta and the knower of the Vedas" (15.15). The chapter closes with the three persons: the perishable (*kshara*), the imperishable (*akshara*), and the Supreme Person (*Purushottama*) who transcends both (15.16-18).

**Osho's reading.** Osho says that most modern thinkers (Darwin, Marx, Bergson, and others) assume the world's development is *upward*, from lower to higher. The Geeta's image of the inverted tree reverses that: the roots are above, in the divine; the branches spread below, in the world. Osho argues that "the more we go ahead, the more we go back; what will be our final destination was our first halt." The world's development is not linear but **circular**. A circle ends where it began. So the first becomes the last, and one who wants to attain the last has to attain a state like the first. "The world is the fall of the divine," he says in what I read, and the only way to grow in the world is for this fall to dissolve and for us to return to the original source. This is why the Geeta and the Upanishads speak of an *upside-down* tree: it is not growth but descent, "the loss of height."

**Why this section matters.** It gives a clear reading of one of the Geeta's most opaque images, and it clarifies why the Geeta's "progress" is a *return*. For Part C: this passage has a deep Vedic ancestry.

### Chapter 16: The divine and demonic qualities
*(Osho: "Acquiring the divine endowment"; read depth: opening discourse.)*

**The Geeta.** Chapter 16, *Daivasura-sampad-vibhaga-yoga*, sets out two kinds of human nature. The **divine endowment** (*daivi sampad*): fearlessness, purity of heart, steadfastness in knowledge and yoga, charity, self-control, sacrifice, study, austerity, uprightness, non-violence, truth, absence of anger, renunciation, tranquillity, absence of fault-finding, compassion, freedom from covetousness, gentleness, modesty, steadiness, vigour, forgiveness, fortitude, purity, freedom from malice and pride (16.1-3). The **demonic endowment** (*asuri sampad*): hypocrisy, arrogance, conceit, anger, harshness, ignorance (16.4). The demonic type says "the world is unreal, without foundation, without God, produced by mutual union with lust for its cause" (16.8), and is "bound by hundreds of cords of desire, given up to lust and anger, striving to amass wealth by unjust means" (16.12). The chapter ends with the **three gates to hell**: lust, anger and greed (16.21), and the instruction that scripture is the authority for what to do and avoid (16.24).

**Osho's reading.** Osho says that man is a *dilemma, a duality*; there is nothing single in him. Light and darkness stand side by side, and "the animal and the divine are both present." Man is like a **ladder**, with one end in hell and one in heaven, and the journey can go in either direction. Importantly, he says, the direction can be reversed at any moment, because it is only a matter of turning. The *same energy* travels in both directions. It would be wrong to suppose a bad man does no *tapasya*; "the bad man also has his austerities, his own great discipline; he too takes great pains." Perhaps his discipline is even harder, Osho says, because the path of evil is full of obstacles for him too, but the good man at the end is rewarded with bliss while the bad man "does not reach anywhere."

**Why this section matters.** It rejects a simple moralism: the difference between saint and sinner is not energy but direction, and direction can change.

### Chapter 17: Faith, and the arithmetic of the three Gunas
*(Osho: "The search for truth and the arithmetic of the three gunas"; read depth: opening discourse.)*

**The Geeta.** Chapter 17, *Shraddhatraya-vibhaga-yoga*, begins with Arjuna's question: what is the status of those who give up the scriptural rules but still worship with faith? Krishna replies that faith (*shraddha*) is of three kinds: sattvic, rajasic, tamasic (17.2). "A man is made of his faith; as is his faith, so is he" (17.3). Sattvic people worship gods; rajasic, demigods and spirits; tamasic, ghosts (17.4). The chapter then classifies three kinds of **food** (17.8-10), **sacrifice** (17.11-13), **austerity** of body, speech and mind (17.14-19), and **charity** (17.20-22). It closes with the classification of the sacred syllable *Om Tat Sat* (17.23-28) and the warning that anything done without faith, "sacrifice, gift or austerity," is called *asat* and is of no use here or hereafter (17.28).

**Osho's reading.** Osho opens with a startling claim: the search for truth is "as old as man; perhaps older," older than the human being. He is, in effect, saying that the aspiration to truth is not something one person invents. Beyond this opening I did not read the discourse, so I can't report how he handles the Geeta's lists of foods, sacrifices, austerities and gifts. The chapter's title in the file, "The search for truth and the arithmetic of the three gunas," tells you he treats the classification as a calculus of the search itself.

**Why this section matters.** The chapter title suggests the lists are read as a *map of the human search* rather than a rulebook, but treat that as my inference from the title.

### Chapter 18: Liberation and renunciation, the final inquiry
*(Osho: "The last inquiry: what is liberation, what is sannyas"; read depth: opening and the closing pages of the whole book.)*

**The Geeta.** Chapter 18, *Moksha-sannyasa-yoga*, is the longest, 78 verses, and gathers up all the themes. Arjuna asks the difference between **sannyasa** (renunciation) and **tyaga** (relinquishment) (18.1). Krishna says that some sages call renunciation of desire-driven actions sannyasa, while the wise call the relinquishment of the *fruits* of all actions tyaga (18.2). He then offers five causes of action (18.14-15), the three kinds of knowledge, action, agent, understanding, steadiness and happiness according to the gunas (18.20-39), and the divisions of social function according to nature (18.41-44), concluding that "one attains perfection by devoting oneself to one's own work" (18.45) and "better one's own dharma, though imperfect, than another's well performed" (18.47). Then the culminating teaching: "Abandoning all dharmas, take refuge in me alone; I will free you from all sins; do not grieve" (18.66). The book ends with Arjuna's declaration, "My delusion is destroyed; I have regained memory through your grace; I stand firm, free from doubt; I will do as you say" (18.73), and Sanjaya's closing verse: "Where there is Krishna, lord of yoga, and where there is Arjuna the archer, there are fortune, victory, prosperity and sure morality" (18.78).

**Osho's reading.** Osho's discourse opens with the elegiac line: "The last chapter of Krishna's Geeta has come. What begins ends." And the final pages of the whole book end with an image that carries the entire teaching of the Gunas to its end.

He urges the listener to leave *tamas* (inertia): "Move a little, shake a little. Bring some motion into life." That is *rajas*, energy, and in rajas "you will see the birth of the many." But then, looking more deeply into each of the many, "you begin to see whether they are truly many or only one." He gives the image of the **sea and its waves**: standing near the sea, many waves appear; looking closer into each wave, it is the same sea, "one sea." "What looks many from outside is one inside. Then the birth of *sattva*, truth, happens." And then the last, decisive move: **don't take even truth as the destination.** "Beyond these three is the *witness* (*sakshi*), and that's why we named it *turiya*, the fourth." *Sattva* (truth) is only an inn. "Take *tamas* as an inn for the night; take *sattva* too as an inn. The bad has to be dropped, but the good has to be dropped too; the lie has to be dropped, but the truth has to be dropped too. For in the end the very grip has to be dropped, in a state where there is neither the one who grips nor anything to grip, only pure awareness." That final state, he says, Mahavira called *kaivalya*, Buddha called *shunya*, Patanjali called *turiya*, and Krishna calls *gunatita*, beyond the gunas.

**Why this section matters.** It ties together the whole book: Osho's last words are an instruction not to stop even at the highest good. The Geeta's own last chapters echo this when they speak of *abandoning all dharmas* (18.66).

# PART C: WHERE THE VEDAS AND THE GEETA MEET

This is the section that justifies putting the three books in one file. The Geeta does not stand outside the Vedas. It is the Vedic tradition arguing with itself, at a moment of crisis, in the voice of one person talking to another.

## 13. The Geeta's relationship to the Vedas: three moves

If you read the Geeta's own statements about the Vedas, they do not add up to simple praise or simple rejection. They make three distinct moves.

### Move 1: Affirmation. The Vedas point to the One.

- "I am to be known by all the Vedas; I am the author of Vedanta and the knower of the Vedas" (15.15).
- "Of the Vedas I am the Sama Veda" (10.22). "Of sacrifices I am the sacrifice of silent repetition (*japa-yajna*)" (10.25). "Of metres I am the Gayatri" (10.35). "Of the Rudras I am Shankara" (10.23). "Of the Adityas I am Vishnu" (10.21). "Of the gods I am Indra" (10.22). Notice that these are the *Vedic* figures: the Gayatri verse and the Sama chant, Agni and Indra and Rudra and the Sun.
- "I am Om in all the Vedas" (7.8; also 9.17).
- "The sage who knows this transcends the fruit of the Vedas, sacrifice, austerity and charity" (8.28).

The affirmation is that the Vedas in their deepest layer are about the same reality that the Geeta calls Krishna, Brahman, the Supreme Person, the Self in all beings. The Rig Veda's own sentence "they call it Indra, Mitra, Varuna, Agni… the One, sages name in many ways" (1.164.46) is, in effect, the premise the Geeta works from.

### Move 2: Criticism. Ritual religion pursued for rewards is not enough.

- Krishna says that some who know the Vedas speak "flowery words" (*pushpita vacham*, 2.42): "Those who are attached to pleasure and power, who say there is nothing else, whose hearts are full of desire and heaven as their goal, utter flowery speech that promises birth as the fruit of action and elaborates many rites for the attainment of enjoyment and lordship" (2.42-43). And then: "The Vedas have the three gunas as their domain; be free of the three gunas, O Arjuna" (2.45). And a famous comparison: "As much use as there is in a well when water floods everywhere, so much is there in all the Vedas for the knower who has seen the Brahman" (2.46).
- In chapter 9 the **trai-vidya**, "the knowers of the three Vedas, who drink the Soma and are purified of sin, worship me by sacrifices and pray for heaven; they reach Indra's holy world and enjoy the celestial pleasures of the gods. Having enjoyed that vast heaven, when their merit is exhausted, they return to the mortal world. Thus those who follow the dharma of the three Vedas, desiring desires, obtain going and returning" (9.20-21). That is a precise description of the Rig Veda's central religion (the Soma sacrifice, the heaven of Indra) and a blunt assessment of its limits: it is a round trip.
- Krishna also says (4.12, 7.20-23) that those who worship other gods for small desires get small, temporary results.

The criticism is not of the Vedas as text. It is of a *use* of the Vedas: sacrifice as a bargain for enjoyment.

### Move 3: Transformation. The sacrifice becomes selfless action.

This is the heart of the matter. The Vedic engine is the yajna cycle: offering → gods → rain → food → beings → offering. The Geeta keeps the whole structure and changes what the offering is.

- "The world is bound by action unless it is done as sacrifice (*yajnarthat karmano 'nyatra loko 'yam karmabandhanah*). Therefore, O son of Kunti, do your action as sacrifice, free from attachment" (3.9).
- "Having created beings together with sacrifice, the Lord of beings said of old: by this shall you multiply; let this be the cow that yields your desires" (3.10).
- "By this foster the gods, and let the gods foster you. Thus fostering one another you shall attain the supreme good" (3.11). This is the Rig Veda's cosmic exchange in two lines.
- "He who does not turn the wheel thus set revolving here lives in vain" (3.16).
- And then the Geeta expands "sacrifice" to cover every action: "Brahman is the offering, Brahman the oblation poured by Brahman into the fire of Brahman; Brahman is reached by him who sees Brahman in all action" (4.24). Other yogis perform sacrifices of knowledge, of the breath, of the senses, of wealth, of austerity, of study (4.25-33).
- The most radical statement: "Whatever you do, whatever you eat, whatever you offer, whatever you give, whatever austerity you practise, O Arjuna, do it as an offering to me" (9.27).

The Yajur Veda's *ritual karma* has become *karma yoga*. Where the Vedic priest said "I offer this butter to Agni," the Geeta's practitioner says "I offer this day's work to the whole." The Vedic "heaven" for the sacrificer is replaced by something no one has to die to enter: freedom, here, from the bondage of action.

Osho's approach to this, in what I read, is not to dwell on the ritual vocabulary but on the psychological result. Krishna's sannyas is not the renunciation of the sacrificial fire (as in the old, outward sannyas that gave up "agni" and the rites) but the renunciation of the *craving for fruit* (his reading of 6.1, in Chapter 6). The "fire" that is given up is internal.

## 14. Seven bridges: concepts that run from the Vedas into the Geeta

### Bridge 1: Rita → Dharma
The Atharva Veda's Hymn to Earth lists *rita* among the pillars that support the earth. In the Geeta, *dharma* is the word doing the same work: "Whenever dharma declines… I manifest myself" (4.7). Osho's first discourse picks up on the thought that the "field of dharma" has failed if it needs a battle. The Rig Veda's Varuna worship was a worship of the moral order that binds; Arjuna's collapse is the collapse of a man who can no longer tell what the order demands.

### Bridge 2: Purusha → Purushottama and the Universal Form
Rig 10.90's thousand-headed, thousand-eyed, thousand-footed Man who "is all that yet hath been and all that is to be" and whose body is the cosmos becomes in the Geeta the figure of **chapter 11**: "with many mouths and eyes, many wondrous sights… the whole universe, standing in one, divided in manifold ways" (11.10-13). The Geeta's chapter 13 has "with hands and feet everywhere, eyes, heads and faces everywhere, with ears everywhere" (13.13), which is an almost word-for-word echo of the Purusha hymn's first verse. And chapter 15 gives the **Purushottama**, the Supreme Person who is beyond both the perishable and the imperishable (15.16-18). The Rig Veda's "one quarter of him are all creatures, three-quarters eternal life in heaven" parallels the Geeta's "I pervade this entire universe with a single fragment of myself" (10.42). The Vedic seer sees the cosmos as a person; Krishna says "see it" and gives Arjuna the eye to do so.

### Bridge 3: Nasadiya → "neither being nor non-being"
The Hymn of Creation says "then was not non-existent nor existent." The Geeta says that the beginningless supreme Brahman "is called neither being nor non-being" (13.12). The Rig Veda's poet ends in honest agnosticism ("he verily knows it, or perhaps he knows not"). The Geeta's Krishna *is* the one who knows, which is the main difference. The poem asks the question; the Geeta, in the person of Krishna, is a response in character.

Osho's tenth-chapter discourse (read depth: opening only) makes a related move from the Western side. The Geeta calls its statements "the supreme word" (*parama vachana*), and Osho notes that logical positivists like Russell, Wittgenstein and Ayer would call statements like "God exists" meaningless since they can be neither verified nor falsified. His answer is that life is a *mystery* and not a *puzzle*. The Nasadiya poet had made much the same discovery three thousand years earlier: at the edge of the question, language itself becomes tentative.

### Bridge 4: Kala (Time) → "I am Time, the destroyer of worlds"
This bridge is the most striking. The Atharva Veda's two Hymns to Time (19.53-54) say that Time is the horse with seven reins who carries all beings, that Time created heaven and earth, that all things are held in Time, that Time was the father of Prajapati (the Creator himself), and "being their father, he became their son; there is, verily, no other force higher than he." In Geeta 11.32 the God, shown in his cosmic form, says "I am Time, the destroyer of worlds, grown vast, engaged here in drawing in the worlds." (*Kalo 'smi lokakshayakrit pravriddho.*) The Atharva Veda's poet gave Time the highest rank among the powers; the Geeta fuses that Time-God with its personal Lord, so that the same being is friend and charioteer *and* the devouring all-powerful Time. The hymns to Time are the oldest source of this identification in the corpus you have.

The same hymn gives you a clue to a puzzle about the Geeta: why does Krishna tell Arjuna that his warriors are "already slain" by Him (11.33-34)? In the Vedic idea of Time the outcome of events is already in the hands of the cosmic power; the human agent is an *instrument* (*nimitta-matram*). This does not remove the human's role to act well, but it removes the burden of being the *ultimate cause*, which is exactly the burden that had crushed Arjuna.

### Bridge 5: Two birds → *Kshetra and Kshetrajna*, and Osho's *sakshi*
The riddle verse of the Rig Veda (1.164.20): two birds, friends, on the same tree; one eats the sweet fruit, the other watches without eating. The Geeta has its field (*kshetra*, the body-mind complex) and its Knower of the field (*kshetrajna*, 13.1-2, 13.22: "the witness, the permitter, the supporter, the experiencer"). Osho's emphasis in the last pages of the whole book is the **witness** (*sakshi*), the "fourth" beyond the three gunas. The same image is presented three times in three centuries: the Rig hymn gives the picture, the Geeta gives the philosophy, and Osho gives it a modern psychological urgency. For Osho the Witness is not a theory but an instruction: *step back from sattva, rajas, tamas, and watch.*

### Bridge 6: Hiranyagarbha / Ka → bhakti and "what God shall we adore?"
The Rig Veda hymn to the unknown God, with its refrain "What God shall we adore with our oblation?", is the poet's *search for an object of devotion* in a crowded pantheon. The Geeta's answer in chapters 9 and 12 is a *named* object: "Fix your mind on me, be devoted to me, sacrifice to me, bow to me; so, united with me, with me as your supreme goal, you shall come to me" (9.34). The "unknown God" of 10.121 becomes the intimate friend, but the Geeta preserves a part of the Rig Veda's reserve: in chapter 12 it concedes that the path toward the unmanifest is "hard for the embodied" (12.5), which is why it offers a path through devotion to someone accessible. Osho's discourse on chapter 12 makes the point from the other side: only the *heart* can enter the realm where the intellect's gate is shut.

### Bridge 7: Savitar and the Gayatri → light, insight, and Osho's reading of the Sun
The Rig Veda's central prayer is to Savitar, the stimulating sun, for insight: *may he impel our thoughts.* The Geeta's chapter 4 opens by saying Krishna first taught the Sun, and Osho reads this as saying the teaching was given to *light*: the first and last event of the inner life is light. The fifteenth chapter echoes: "The light of the sun that illumines the entire world, the light in the moon and in fire, know that light to be mine" (15.12). Osho's reading connects the Vedic prayer for illumination with the Geeta's claim that truth is *already present*, "like the sun that is already there; you only open the door."

## 15. Where the Geeta departs from the Vedas

The Geeta is a continuation of the Vedic tradition but not a copy. A few real differences, to keep the picture honest:

1. **Heaven is not the goal.** The Samhitas ask for a place in the fathers' heaven. The Geeta says heaven is temporary (9.21) and aims instead at *moksha*, release from the cycle altogether (the title of chapter 18).
2. **Birth and class.** The Purusha hymn's fourfold division of society (10.90.12) is mirrored in the Geeta's own mention of the four orders (4.13; 18.41-44), but the Geeta states that these are divided by *guna and karma*, quality and work, not by birth alone (4.13, and the whole of chapter 18's reasoning about each person's *svabhava*, one's own nature). Readers and commentators have interpreted these verses very differently, from defending hereditary hierarchy to arguing that the verse describes aptitude. I did not read Osho's treatment of these verses in the part of the book I covered, and I do not attribute a view to him.
3. **Ritual precision is no longer the point.** The Yajur Veda's exact formulas (right word, right movement, right substance) are replaced by the *attitude* of the doer. The Geeta even says that whatever is offered with love, "a leaf, a flower, a fruit, water," is accepted (9.26).
4. **A personal God.** The Vedic gods are many and impersonal forces. The Geeta gives you one voice, with a face, who speaks in the first person, who says "you are dear to me" (18.64). The Upanishads had said the Self is Brahman; the Geeta lets Brahman *talk* to a friend.
5. **A crisis.** The Samhitas are not written to someone in the middle of a moral collapse. The Geeta is. That changes the tone: each Vedic hymn asks for something; the Geeta begins with someone who has stopped being able to ask.

## 17. What Osho adds

A Part C synthesis would be incomplete without saying what Osho brings that is distinct. From what I read, five things.

1. **Psychology as the translator.** He turns the old words into the idiom of Freud, Adler, and the existentialists. The Vedic *tapas* (fervour) becomes the energy that both good and bad people use; the *sthitaprajna's* firmness becomes the problem of masks; the *gunas* become temperaments we can watch. The Vedic world has no word for "repression," yet Osho makes Krishna's *mudha* (the fool who suppresses the senses) the first psychologist.
2. **The priority of experience over borrowed knowledge.** Over and over, he says a conclusion has to be *digested*. That is a distinctive emphasis. The Geeta's text ends with Arjuna saying "I will do as you say" (18.73); I did not read Osho's comment on that verse, so I can't say how he squares it with his stress on self-made decisions.
3. **A strong reading of sannyas.** For Osho, Krishna's contribution to world religion is the idea that renunciation can be *inward*. In the opening of chapter 6 he contrasts the old, life-negating renunciation (leave home, work, life) with Krishna's inner one (drop the craving for fruit). That reading is supported by Geeta 6.1 and 5.3.
4. **Symbolic reading of myth.** The Sun, the field of Kurukshetra, the blind king, the chariot, the inverted tree: all are treated as psychological or spiritual symbols, not as events in history. This is consistent with a long tradition of symbolic readings of the Veda itself.
5. **The last move, past all qualities.** The last pages say the three gunas, including the highest, *sattva*, are inns to leave. The witness is the fourth. This takes the Geeta beyond ethics to a state that Osho says Mahavira, Buddha, Patanjali and Krishna all point to with different words. It is a distinctly Osho emphasis, and also a faithful reading of the Geeta's own word *gunatita*.

## 18. Ten core ideas, if you remember nothing else

1. The Vedas are four collections: Rig (praise), Yajur (ritual formulas), Sama (song), Atharva (charms and mystery).
2. The Rig Veda's world is a ritual exchange between humans and gods through fire, in service of cosmic order (*rita*).
3. Inside the Vedas themselves a shift occurs from "many gods" to "the One by many names" (1.164.46) to the Primeval Man (10.90), the Golden Germ (10.121), the Creation hymn (10.129), and Time and Breath in the Atharva.
4. The Geeta takes up the sacrificial pattern and turns it into selfless action: *yajna* becomes any act offered without attachment to the fruit.
5. The Geeta criticizes the use of the Vedas for the purchase of heaven, since heaven is temporary.
6. The self is unborn and deathless (2.20); what we have lost is not the truth but the *remembrance* of it (Osho on chapter 3).
7. Real renunciation is internal: Krishna's sannyas is the renunciation of craving, not of life (chapter 5 and 6).
8. Suppression of the senses backfires; the mind is the issue, not the senses (Osho on 3.6).
9. The universe is a person and a process: Purusha in the Rig Veda, the Universal Form in chapter 11, Time in the Atharva Veda and in 11.32.
10. The last step is beyond even the best quality: the witness who is not bound by sattva, rajas or tamas (Osho on chapter 18).

## 21. Common misunderstandings, and what the texts actually say

1. **"The Vedas are one religion's holy book with one message."** They are four collections composed over centuries and by many voices, with a progression from praise and petition to speculation. The Rig Veda itself contains a hymn that says the gods came *after* creation (10.129.6) and a verse that says the many gods are names for one (1.164.46).
2. **"The Vedas teach caste."** The only direct Rig Vedic text for the four orders is 10.90.12, which tradition debates. Modern scholars note it appears in the latest Mandala and is often thought to be a late insertion; the earlier family books do not describe hereditary castes in the later sense. The Geeta says the four orders are divided by *guna and karma* (4.13). Interpreters have taken these passages in sharply different directions, and I have not covered Osho's view of them.
3. **"The Geeta rejects the Vedas."** It criticizes the *use* of the Vedas to purchase heaven (2.42-46, 9.20-21) but also says "I am to be known by all the Vedas" (15.15) and honours the Gayatri, Sama and Om. Its attitude is "go through them, then through to what they point at."
4. **"Krishna tells Arjuna to kill because killing doesn't matter."** The argument in chapter 2 is about the deathless self, but the chapter is not a licence for violence. It is part of a larger argument that Arjuna has a duty he cannot evade by running away, followed by sixteen chapters that spell out *how* to act: without hatred, without ego, without craving fruit, with surrender. Osho reads the war as a metaphor for the inner battle, and spends his first discourses on the king's blindness and on dharma's failing, not on the morality of killing.
5. **"Renunciation means leaving home."** Chapter 6's first verse, and Osho's discourse on it, say the opposite: the real sannyasi is one who acts without craving, not one who has stopped acting.
6. **"Karma means fate."** In the Vedas it means *ritual action*. In the Geeta it means action in general. The Geeta's innovation is that the fruit of action is not what binds; the attachment to it is.
7. **"Bhakti, jnana and karma are three rival paths."** The Geeta treats them as three emphases of one path, and Osho reads them as three doors into the same room: heart (12), knowledge (13), action (3).
8. **"The unmanifest and the personal God are opposed."** Chapter 12 starts with exactly that question and answers that both reach him, but that the path of the unmanifest is "harder for the embodied." The Geeta does not say the unmanifest is wrong, only that devotion is more accessible.
9. **"The Atharva Veda is just magic."** It does contain charms and exorcisms, but also the Hymn to Earth, the hymns to Time, Breath and the Support, and it was a major source for early Indian medicine, politics and philosophy.
10. **"The 2002 compilation is a neutral translation."** It declares in its introduction that it has a programme (a unified book for practitioners and a "Global Dharmic Renaissance"), and it replaces some old verses with its own versions in places. It is still useful because it unifies the numbering, but use it with Book 1 when you need a literal reading.

## 22. Glossary

**Adhidaiva**: the divine aspect of the world (8.1-4). **Adhiyajna**: the one who presides over sacrifice. **Advaita**: non-dualism; "not two." **Agni**: fire; priest and messenger of the gods. **Ahimsa**: non-violence. **Akarma**: non-action; action without the doer's grip. **Aksara / Akshara**: the imperishable. **Asat / Sat**: non-being / being (Rig 10.129; Geeta 13.12). **Ashvattha**: the sacred fig, the inverted tree of chapter 15. **Asura**: in early texts a powerful lord (Varuna); later a demon. **Atman**: the self. **Avatara**: descent, incarnation (4.7). **Bhakti**: devotion. **Brahman**: the Absolute; in early Vedic use, the sacred power of the utterance; *brahmana*: a priest/knower. **Brahmacharin**: a student of sacred knowledge (Atharva 11.5). **Buddhi**: intellect, discernment. **Deva**: god, shining one. **Dharma**: that which upholds; Geeta 11.32). **Kama**: desire (Rig 10.129.4; Atharva 9.2). **Karma**: action; in the Vedas ritual action. **Kshetra**: the field (body-mind). **Kshetrajna**: the knower of the field. **Maya**: the creative power that makes the world appear; ## 24. A reading plan if you want to go deeper yourself

**Week 1: the Rig Veda.** Read Hymns 1.1, 1.35, 1.164 (only the verses quoted here), 7.59 (the Tryambaka verse), 10.90, 10.121, 10.129, and 10.190. Then sample Book 9 (Soma Pavamana) and two of the Indra hymns to see the ritual religion in full voice.

**Week 2: the other three.** Read the first page of the Yajur Veda and the Offerings to Rudra; the first two decades of the Sama Veda and Griffith's preface; the Atharva's Hymn to Earth, Prana, Kama and the two Hymns to Time.

**Week 3: the Geeta in Sanskrit-English.** Read the eighteen chapters in a short translation, a chapter a night, before you open Osho. Underline the verses cited in this guide's Section 14.

**Week 4: Osho.** Read the opening discourse of each chapter; return to the long passages on the senses (chapters 1-2), on the Sun (chapter 4), on the new sannyas (chapter 6), on effort and grace (chapter 11), and the last pages of chapter 18.

## 25. Honest summary of limitations, and what I would do next

I have given you a complete *map* of three very large books and a reasonably deep reading of their best-known passages. I have not read every hymn, every Yajur formula, every Atharva charm, or every one of Osho's roughly 3,000 pages. A few results to keep in mind:

- The structure and counts I report for each Veda are from the books' own tables of contents and introductions. Where the two editions disagree (the 2002 compilation says six Yajur kandhas and Keith counts seven; the 2002 text has aspirational hymn counts; Griffith's Mandala 2 has 43 hymns while the compilation lists 48), I have flagged it.
- The Hindi text extraction is imperfect. Where I paraphrase Osho, I paraphrase what I could read; I could not read his Chapter 8 discourse closely, and for chapters 3 to 17 I read mostly the first discourse of each.
- Osho's commentary is a distinct voice with its own emphases. This guide is not a neutral scholarly view of the Geeta; it is the Geeta as Osho reads it, set beside the Vedic text.
- The Vedic translations are old (1890s to 1910s), and in a few places the 2002 compilation swapped in newer wording. For serious scholarly work on any hymn you would want a modern critical translation.

**If you want me to take this further,** the most useful next steps would be (a) a deep, chapter-by-chapter reading of one Osho volume at a time, so the thinner chapters (3, 7, 8, 14, 16, 17) get the same depth as chapters 1 and 2; (b) a per-Mandala reading of the Rig Veda with a short summary of every hymn; or (c) a separate comparison file focused only on how Osho's reading differs from traditional commentators like Shankara, Ramanuja and Tilak.
